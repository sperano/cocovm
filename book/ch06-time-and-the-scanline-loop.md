# Chapter 6 — Time: the scanline loop, sync pulses, and "it's alive"

*Week 6. Goal: understand `run_field()` — the heartbeat every other chapter
plugs into. Chapters 2–4 gave you a CPU that can execute any instruction
correctly; Chapter 5 gave you a bus that resolves any address correctly. Neither
one, on its own, produces a running machine — a CPU with a bus is just a
function you can call, not something that boots. This week adds the missing
ingredient: a clock. By the end you will know exactly how many CPU cycles
fit in one scanline on real NTSC hardware, and why the loop is built to be
paused and resumed one instruction at a time. Then comes the payoff: a real
1980s ROM booting to a prompt and answering `PRINT 2+2`, from cold reset,
with nothing but this loop driving it.*

---

Five weeks of work have produced something that is, strictly speaking, not
yet a computer. It is a very good *calculator*: hand it an opcode and it
will tell you, correctly and repeatably, what the 6809 would have done with
that opcode, down to the flag bits and the cycle count. Hand it an address
and the bus will tell you which of RAM, ROM, or a device register the CoCo
would have selected. Every fact in the machine is right. What is missing is
the one thing that turns a pile of correct facts into a running system:
*something has to decide when each of those facts happens*.

That is a bigger gap than it sounds. A CoCo 3 sitting at the BASIC prompt
is not idle in any interesting sense — it is running an interrupt handler
sixty times a second that blinks a cursor, scans a keyboard matrix, and
returns to a two-byte loop whose only job is to be interrupted again. Take
the clock away and every one of those events either never happens or
happens all at once. The difference between "the machine boots" and "the
machine is a frozen screen full of garbage" is entirely a question of
timing. Chapter 6 is where the emulator stops being a library and starts being
a machine.

The chapter is a guided tour of one file. `crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs`
is under two hundred lines including its comments, and it contains the
entire timing model: the field loop, the resumable per-instruction
primitive underneath it, the priority order in which HALT, interrupts, and
the CPU get their turn, and the per-scanline trailer where video, audio,
sync pulses, and the GIME's interval timer all get fed. Along the way the
chapter does the arithmetic that produces the loop's magic numbers, follows
a single sync pulse through four function calls until it lands in the CPU's
IRQ vector, and — since this file is what a debugger drives — takes a
detour into `debug.rs` to see what "resumable" actually buys.

Two things make this week different from the four before it. The first is
that everything here is *observable*: the sections that make claims about
instruction counts and interrupt cadence back them with numbers produced by
actually running the code, not by reasoning about it. The second is that
the payoff arrives at the end of the chapter rather than the end of the
course. Section 6.8 boots a real 1980s ROM to a prompt, types at it, and
asserts on the answer, in a headless `cargo test` that finishes in a
fraction of a second.

## 6.1 Why an emulator needs a clock at all

Everything since Chapter 1 has been about *correctness*: given an instruction
and a bus, produce the right result. Nothing so far has asked *when*.
`cpu.step(&mut bus)` doesn't know or care whether it's being called by a
test harness, a debugger's single-step button, or a video game running at
60 frames a second. It executes one instruction and returns however many
cycles that instruction cost, and its obligations end there.

Call that function in a bare `loop {}` and it will execute billions of
instructions per second on modern hardware, because nothing is stopping it.
The real 6809E in a CoCo 3 executed 894,886 of them per second, no more and
no less, because its clock pin was wired to a crystal that oscillates at a
fixed rate. Silicon has no choice about pacing; software has nothing *but*
choices, and unless it makes one deliberately it will pick the fastest one
available.

An emulator that spins `cpu.step` in a tight loop therefore computes the
right *values* in the wrong *amount of time*, which sounds like an academic
complaint until you look at what the CoCo actually used those cycles for.
Everything a person could see or hear coming out of the machine was
generated by watching cycles go by. The video hardware painted the screen
sixty times a second by counting cycles per scanline. The sound DAC's
waveform *was* a sequence of writes at particular moments; the moments were
the sound. Keyboard debouncing assumed a particular scan rate, and the
cassette port's entire encoding scheme was built out of tone durations
measured in CPU cycles.

Decouple the cycle count from wall-clock time and the machine you get isn't
a fast CoCo. It's a different, wrong machine — one where the cursor blinks
at ten thousand hertz, tape loads decode to garbage, and every note the
sound chip plays is the same inaudible click. Correctness in an emulator
has a time axis, and this is the week the emulator grows one.

### Two clocks that have to agree

Something, then, has to tie CPU cycles to real time. Two somethings,
actually. The interesting part is that they are almost unrelated to each
other, yet both have to be satisfied by the same loop.

The first is the video hardware's own sync pulses. Real CRT electronics
need a horizontal sync pulse at the end of every scanline, to snap the
electron beam back to the left edge of the screen, and a vertical or field
sync pulse sixty times a second to snap it back to the top. The CoCo's
chips generate both of these off the same crystal that clocks the CPU, and
— this is the fact this chapter spends the most time on — some of those
pulses are wired directly into the CPU's interrupt lines. The 60 Hz
field-sync interrupt is not cosmetic and it is not optional. Stock BASIC's
main loop is *waiting* for it, and §6.6 tells the story of what an emulator
looks like when it doesn't arrive.

The second is the host's own real-time clock. Whatever machine you're
running the emulator on has to present roughly sixty finished video fields
per second to a human watching it. Miss that and the emulation feels wrong
in a way no amount of internal accuracy can compensate for: too fast, too
slow, or — the worst of the three — choppy, where the average rate is
correct but the individual fields arrive in clumps.

The scanline is the natural unit that satisfies both at once, and it is
worth being explicit about why, because "run one field at a time" and "run
one instruction at a time" are both defensible alternatives. A scanline is
small enough to schedule video and audio work accurately, because a real
CoCo's picture can change *within* a field and not just between fields —
the screen-split effects that are Chapter 9's subject depend on exactly that.
And a whole field's worth of scanlines is precisely the quantum the host's
60 Hz repaint wants to be handed. One unit, both constraints.

### The design sketch, and what survived it

`DESIGN.md` §4 committed to this model before any of the code existed, and
its sketch of the loop is short enough to quote in full. Read it as a
statement of intent rather than as a description of the finished code —
several details did not survive contact with the hardware, and noticing
which ones is a large part of this chapter:

```
run_field():
  for line in 0..lines_per_field:           // NTSC 262 @ 60Hz, PAL 312 @ 50Hz
      run CPU for ~CYCLES_PER_LINE cycles    (≈ 57 @ 0.895 MHz; ~same per-line both standards)
      gime.tick_timer(by cycles, line)       // 12-bit countdown
      if line in active area: gime.render_scanline(line, ram, fb)
      gime.hsync()    // may raise HBORD interrupt
  gime.vsync()        // may raise VBORD interrupt; present framebuffer
```

The overall shape is exactly what got built. Lines are the outer unit, a
slice of CPU cycles runs inside each one, and a trailer at the end of the
line ticks the timer, renders, and pulses the horizontal sync. Three
details in that sketch, though, turned out to be wrong or imprecise, and
each of them gets its own section below. The `≈ 57` cycles-per-line figure
is off by one for NTSC, for a reason that has nothing to do with the
hardware and everything to do with integer division (§6.2). The single
`gime.vsync()` call at the bottom of the loop is not how CoCo field sync
works at all — it is two separate edges at two specific scanlines in the
middle of the field (§6.5). And the `for line in ...` loop itself is gone, replaced by
something that can be stopped and resumed between any two instructions
(§6.3).

None of those corrections is a criticism of the sketch. A design document
that is right about everything was written after the fact. What makes this
one useful is that the corrections are still attached to it, so you can
watch a plan meet a machine and lose three arguments.

[`crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs)
is where the surviving design actually lives, and this chapter reads that
file end to end.

### One method that is not the loop

Before starting, there is one piece of housekeeping to get out of the way,
because it will otherwise cause confusion the first time you grep this
codebase. There is a method called
plain `Machine::step()`, and it is *not* the loop this chapter is about.
Here it is in its entirety, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs:240-243`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs#L240-L243):

```rust
/// Execute one CPU instruction; returns cycles consumed.
pub fn step(&mut self) -> u32 {
    self.cpu.step(&mut self.bus)
}
```

That is a one-line wrapper around the CPU's own step function with none of
the scanline, interrupt, or peripheral bookkeeping this chapter is about.
It exists for raw instruction-trace tooling that wants the CPU and nothing
else, and you will find it used in exactly three places:
[`examples/trace.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/examples/trace.rs),
[`tests/boot.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/boot.rs), and
[`tests/snapshot_roundtrip.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/snapshot_roundtrip.rs).

It is genuinely useful for what it does and genuinely dangerous for
anything else, because a machine driven by `Machine::step` will never take
an interrupt as long as it runs — no scanline boundary is ever crossed, so
no sync pulse is ever emitted, so PIA0's interrupt flag never latches.
Every real boot in this codebase goes through `run_field` and
`step_instruction` instead. If you ever find yourself with a machine that
executes flawlessly and does nothing, check which of the two you called.

## 6.2 The numbers, derived

The run loop is built out of two integers: how many CPU cycles fit in a
video field, and how many fit in one scanline. Both are computed rather
than written down, in four lines of code, from three constants and one
boolean. Derive them by hand first. The code is going to look arbitrary
otherwise, and — more importantly — the arithmetic
contains one small trap that produces a number differing from the design
document's by exactly one, which is the most instructive kind of
discrepancy there is.

### The CPU clock

The starting point is the constant Chapter 1 already introduced, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs:24-26`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs#L24-L26):

```rust
/// NTSC CPU clock at normal speed: the 28.636363 MHz crystal / 32 (MAME
/// `coco3.cpp`). The SAM R1 bit doubles it (crystal / 16, ~1.79 MHz).
const CPU_HZ: f64 = 894_886.0;
```

That number is a fixture of the platform's literature — 0.895 MHz is the
figure every CoCo reference quotes, and it is odd enough that people who
have never thought about it assume it was rounded from something cleaner.
It wasn't. It comes from dividing the NTSC colorburst-derived crystal, at
28.636363 MHz, by 32. That crystal is itself eight times the 3.579545 MHz
NTSC color subcarrier, which is the frequency the video output has to
respect if color is going to survive the trip to a television set.

The direction of causation is worth stating plainly, because it explains
every strange constant in this chapter. Nobody chose a CPU speed and then
found a crystal to produce it. Somebody chose a *television standard*, the
crystal followed from the color subcarrier, and the CPU clock is what
comes out of a divide-by-32 on the tail end of that decision. Video timing,
CPU timing, cassette baud rates, and the GIME's fast timer clock are all
integer relationships to one frequency picked for compatibility with
broadcast television. That shared origin is what lets the entire machine be
driven off a single cycle counter without any subsystem drifting away from
the others.

### Field rate and lines per field

The second and third constants describe the picture rather than the
processor, and they live on the `VideoStandard` enum, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/config.rs:48-63`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/config.rs#L48-L63):

```rust
impl VideoStandard {
    /// Scanlines per field. Provisional (standard video values; see `DESIGN.md` §4).
    pub const fn lines_per_field(self) -> u32 {
        match self {
            VideoStandard::NTSC => 262,
            VideoStandard::PAL => 312,
        }
    }

    /// Nominal field (refresh) rate in Hz.
    pub const fn field_rate_hz(self) -> f64 {
        match self {
            VideoStandard::NTSC => 59.94,
            VideoStandard::PAL => 50.0,
        }
    }
}
```

262 lines at roughly sixty fields per second is standard-definition NTSC.
It is the same 262 — or 263, split across two interlaced fields, which this
emulator does not model — that every NTSC console and computer of the era
inherited from broadcast television, for the same reason they all inherited
the color subcarrier: that was what the television on the other end of the
cable expected.

The `59.94` deserves a second look, because a reader who half-remembers the
number will want to correct it to 60. It is not a typo and it is not an
approximation of 60. Color NTSC's frame rate is exactly `30000/1001` frames
per second, a value chosen in the early 1950s so that color broadcasts
would remain watchable on the monochrome sets already in living rooms.
Doubled for fields, that is 59.94 Hz. Eighty years later it is still a
number emulator authors have to type correctly, because getting it wrong by
a tenth of a percent quietly shifts every cycle budget derived from it.

Notice also that both methods are `const fn`, which means the compiler can
evaluate them at compile time when the argument is known. That is not doing
any heavy lifting in the run loop — these get called through a runtime
`self.config.video` — but it does document the intent: these are constants
wearing a function's clothes, not computations.

### Cycles per field, then cycles per line

Now put the pieces together the way `cycles_per_field` does, which is the
clock divided by the field rate:

```
894_886.0 / 59.94  =  14929.09...  →  truncated to u32: 14929 cycles/field
```

Cycles per *line* is that number divided by lines per field. This is where
you have to be careful, because the code does two separate truncating
integer divisions rather than one clean one. `cycles_per_field()` returns a
`u32`, so the fractional `.09` is discarded before anything else happens,
and it is that already-truncated 14929 that gets divided by
`lines_per_field()` at
[`run.rs:48`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L48),
quoted in full in §6.3:

```
14929 / 262  =  56.98...  →  truncated: 56 cycles/line
```

So the answer is 56, not 57. The design sketch in §6.1 said "≈ 57 @ 0.895
MHz," and it was not being careless: divide the CPU clock by the NTSC
horizontal rate of 15734 Hz and you get 56.9, which rounds to 57 and is
correct to within a rounding convention. The emulator, though, does not
round. It floors, twice, and lands on 56.

This is a good habit to build right now, in Chapter 6, before you have
internalized which numbers in this codebase are load-bearing constants and
which are conversational approximations in a comment. When a design
document and the source disagree by one, trust the source and then go find
out why. Here the "why" is boring — flooring two already-floored quantities
loses a fraction each time — but "boring, and checked" is a completely
different state of knowledge from "probably fine." It is also exactly the
kind of question the debugger in Chapter 16 exists to let you settle by
inspection rather than by squinting at decimal points.

> **Rust corner: `as` on a float truncates, and never panics.** The line
> that produces 14929 is `(hz / self.config.video.field_rate_hz()) as u32`,
> and the `as` cast is doing more work than it looks like. Casting `f64` to
> an integer type in Rust truncates toward zero — it does not round, and it
> does not round-half-to-even. `14929.09_f64 as u32` is 14929, and
> `-1.9_f64 as i32` is `-1`, not `-2`.
>
> The cast is also *saturating*, which is the part worth remembering. A
> float larger than `u32::MAX` clamps to `u32::MAX` instead of wrapping or
> invoking undefined behavior, and `NaN` casts to `0`. That is a
> deliberate departure from C, where the same conversion is undefined
> behavior outside the target's range and has produced real security bugs.
> Rust made the conversion total at a small cost in generated code, which
> is the right trade for a language that wants `as` to be a safe operation.
>
> None of the pathological cases can arise here — the operands are a fixed
> clock and a fixed field rate — but knowing that `as` floors rather than
> rounds is precisely what turns the 56-versus-57 question from a mystery
> into arithmetic. When you want rounding, `f64::round()` first and cast
> after; when you want the compiler to stop you from guessing, don't use
> `as` at all and reach for `TryFrom`.

### PAL, for comparison

PAL runs at the same CPU clock and produces slightly different numbers. The
CoCo 1 and CoCo 2 don't support PAL in this emulator at all —
`MachineConfig::validate` rejects the combination outright at
[`config.rs:213-217`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/config.rs#L213-L217)
— so every PAL figure below describes a CoCo 3 in practice. The arithmetic
is still worth seeing, because the way the two standards nearly cancel out
is the whole reason the design sketch could claim they were the same:

```
894_886.0 / 50.0  =  17897 cycles/field  (truncated)
17897 / 312  =  57 cycles/line  (truncated)
```

PAL's per-line number, 57, is closer to the sketch's "≈57" than NTSC's
actual 56 is, which is a nice reminder that "same per-line for both
standards" is approximately true rather than exactly true.

### All four combinations at once

Cross the two `VideoStandard` variants with the two `cpu_fast` states and
the whole timing model fits in one table. Everything the run loop knows
about time is derived from `CPU_HZ`, each standard's `lines_per_field` and
`field_rate_hz`, and a single boolean:

| Standard | Speed | `lines_per_field()` | `field_rate_hz()` | cycles/field (`hz / rate`, truncated) | cycles/line (`÷ lines`, truncated) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| NTSC | normal | 262 | 59.94 | 14929 | **56** |
| NTSC | fast (`POKE 65497,0`) | 262 | 59.94 | 29859 | **113** |
| PAL | normal | 312 | 50.0 | 17897 | **57** |
| PAL | fast | 312 | 50.0 | 35795 | **114** |

Three observations fall out of seeing them lined up like this rather than
scattered across paragraphs, and each one answers a question that otherwise
nags.

The first is that the field-rate column, not the line-count column, is what
makes PAL "slower per line." The intuition most people start with is that
PAL has more lines to draw — 312 against 262 — so each one must get a
smaller share of the CPU. But PAL also runs at a proportionally lower field
rate, 50 Hz against 59.94, which means each field has *more* cycles to
spread across those extra lines: 17897 against NTSC's 14929. The two
effects nearly cancel, and the second one wins by a hair. This is why the
design document could claim the "horizontal rate is nearly identical for
both" despite the two field rates differing by twenty percent. Broadcast
engineers on two continents independently tuned their standards to land
near the same 15.6–15.7 kHz horizontal rate, because the deflection
circuitry in a television set is the expensive part and nobody wanted to
build two of them. The near-cancellation in this table is an inheritance
from that, not something the codebase manufactured.

The second observation is that doubling the CPU speed does not cleanly
double the per-line number, and whether it happens to come out even is
standard-dependent. NTSC fast is 113, one cycle above a clean double of 56.
PAL fast is 114, *exactly* double PAL's 57. Nothing in the code
special-cases either standard to make that happen. It is the same
double-truncation arithmetic applied to different real-number inputs:
NTSC's untruncated line value of 56.98 sits just below 57, so doubling the
untruncated field value and truncating afterwards preserves more of that
fraction than doubling the already-floored 56 would have.
Truncate-then-double and double-then-truncate can disagree by one, and
which side of the disagreement each configuration lands on is arithmetic
accident rather than tuning.

The third observation is the practically important one, and it is about
what the loop *doesn't* know. Nothing in `step_instruction` or
`end_of_line` — the two functions §6.3 through §6.5 dissect — branches on
`VideoStandard` at all. Every line of the run loop reads `line`,
`line_cycles_spent`, and `line_budget`, three plain `u32`s that are already
standard-agnostic and speed-agnostic by the time the loop sees them. All
four rows of that table collapse into the same three fields. The video
standard and the speed bit matter at exactly one point in the entire
codebase, which is `cycles_per_field`, and that is what the next subsection
is about.

### The double-speed poke

`POKE 65497,0` is one of the two or three pieces of shared CoCo folklore
that everybody who used the machine knows: type it and BASIC programs, disk
accesses, and the cursor blink all get noticeably snappier, and some
programs promptly stop working. What it actually does is flip the CoCo 3's
SAM-compatible R1 speed-select strobe, and the test that pins the behavior
down is
[`crates/coco-core/tests/speed.rs:1-9`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/speed.rs#L1-L9),
whose header and address constants say most of it before a single test runs
(its `use` lines are omitted here):

```rust
//! SAM R1 CPU-rate strobe ($FFD8/$FFD9 — `POKE 65497,0`): true double speed
//! on the CoCo 3. R0 ($FFD6/$FFD7) is inert (SEB Unravelled II Fig 8).

const R0_SET: u16 = 0xFFD7;
const R1_CLEAR: u16 = 0xFFD8;
const R1_SET: u16 = 0xFFD9;
```

`0xFFD9` is decimal 65497, which is where the folklore comes from. Writing
*any* value there sets `gime.cpu_fast`, because SAM strobes care only that
you touched the address and not what byte you wrote — Chapter 5's "weird
hardware" moment, back again. Writing to `0xFFD8`, decimal 65496, clears it.

There is a wrinkle worth knowing, because the folklore is slightly wrong
about the other half of the pair. The speed poke is often quoted as the
pair `65497`/`65495`, meaning `$FFD9` and `$FFD7`, and `$FFD7` is R0, which
SEB Unravelled II's Figure 8 documents as wired but inert on real CoCo 3
hardware. This emulator models that inertness faithfully rather than
quietly accepting both addresses: the test
`r1_strobes_latch_cpu_speed_and_r0_is_inert` at
[`speed.rs:12-25`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/speed.rs#L12-L25)
deliberately writes `R0_SET` last and then asserts the speed is *still* off.
So if you go looking for the "real" off-poke, `$FFD8` and decimal 65496 are
the ones that do something here; 65495 is not.

Once `cpu_fast` is set, the one function that reads it is `cycles_per_field`,
at
[`run.rs:185-195`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L185-L195):

```rust
fn cycles_per_field(&self) -> u32 {
    // Speed-poke source differs per variant: the GIME's own R1 latch on
    // CoCo 3, the plain SAM's R0|R1 strobes on CoCo 1/2
    // (`docs/coco12-plan.md` Phase 4; `Sam::cpu_fast`'s KNOWN GAP note).
    let cpu_fast = match self.config.variant {
        MachineVariant::Coco3 => self.bus.gime.cpu_fast,
        MachineVariant::Coco1 | MachineVariant::Coco2 => self.bus.sam.cpu_fast(),
    };
    let hz = if cpu_fast { CPU_HZ * 2.0 } else { CPU_HZ };
    (hz / self.config.video.field_rate_hz()) as u32
}
```

Four lines of logic, and every number in the table above comes out of them.
The `match` on `self.config.variant` is Chapter 1's family-history theme in
miniature: the speed bit lives in the GIME's own latch on a CoCo 3 and in
the plain SAM chip on a CoCo 1 or 2, two different pieces of silicon
answering the same software request, and the code keeps both rather than
pretending one of them covers the other. Then the boolean picks a clock,
the clock gets divided by the field rate, and the `as u32` truncates.

Doubling `CPU_HZ` and re-running the same division gives
`(894_886.0 * 2.0) / 59.94 = 29859` cycles per field and `29859 / 262 = 113`
cycles per line, a hair over exactly double 56, for the double-truncation
reason already established. The companion test,
[`tests/speed.rs::speed_poke_doubles_cycles_per_field`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/speed.rs),
is written with that slop in mind: it parks the CPU on `BRA *`, counts
cycles retired in one field before and after the poke, and asserts the
ratio lands in `1.9..=2.1` rather than pinning an exact integer. Asserting
"about twice as many" is the honest assertion when the underlying quantity
is two floors deep.

Step back and look at what the whole double-speed feature amounts to from
the emulator's point of view. It is one boolean changing which branch a
single multiplication takes. Everything downstream — `line_budget`, the
scanline trailer, the GIME timer's tick multiplier, the audio grid — is
completely unaware that anything changed. None of them ever asks about
speed; they ask about cycles, and the cycle budget already has the answer
baked in. That is the dividend of routing every timing decision
through one function instead of scattering `if fast { ... }` checks through
the loop, and it is worth noticing now, because §6.10's exercise 6.1 has
you redo this arithmetic by hand and it will go much faster if you know
there is only one place to look.

## 6.3 The loop anatomy: `run_field`, `step_instruction`, resumability

With the numbers established, the loop itself can be read for what it is
rather than decoded. Open
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs).
The file-level doc comment announces the shape before you have read a line
of code, and it is worth taking as a table of contents for the next three
sections:

```rust
//! The scanline-driven run loop: `run_field`/`step_instruction` (the
//! resumable per-instruction primitive both are built on), the per-cycle
//! peripheral tick, the per-line trailer, and interrupt servicing.
```

Four things, in the order this chapter covers them. The word to notice is
*resumable*, which appears in the first sentence and turns out to be the
organizing constraint on everything else in the file.

### `run_field`, in one line

`run_field` is the function every other chapter of this course calls to
make the machine do anything, and it is almost insultingly short. Its doc
comment is four times the length of its body, and both are worth reading;
here is the doc comment first, from
[`run.rs:10-19`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L10-L19):

```rust
/// Run one video field's worth of emulation (`DESIGN.md` §4).
///
/// Scanline-driven: each line runs a slice of CPU cycles then pulses the
/// horizontal sync. The field (vertical) sync is two separate edges at
/// their own scanlines mid-field — not one pulse at the end of the loop
/// — per [`crate::config::VideoStandard::fs_falling_line`] /
/// [`crate::config::VideoStandard::fs_rising_line`]. All of these are wired to
/// PIA0 and drive the CPU IRQ, which is delivered between instructions —
/// this is what breaks the stock ROM out of its idle loop and runs
/// BASIC's housekeeping. Video scanout is filled at the end (`§6`).
```

That comment is the design sketch from §6.1 with its corrections already
folded in. "Not one pulse at the end of the loop" is a direct repudiation
of the sketch's `gime.vsync()` line, written by someone who had to find
out. And the sentence about the CPU IRQ being "what breaks the stock ROM
out of its idle loop" is the entire war story of §6.6 compressed to a
single clause.

The body, at
[`run.rs:20-27`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L20-L27),
is one statement:

```rust
pub fn run_field(&mut self) {
    // Resumable equivalent of the old nested scanline/cycle loops: drive
    // `step_instruction` from wherever the machine is parked until a field
    // completes. On a fresh machine (or right after any previous field —
    // both leave `line`/`line_cycles_spent` at 0) this is exactly one full
    // field from line 0, byte-for-byte identical to the pre-refactor loop.
    while !self.step_instruction().field_complete {}
}
```

A `while` loop with an empty body, calling one method until it reports
`true`. Everything interesting — line budgets, HALT, interrupts, horizontal
sync, field sync, audio, the GIME timer — lives inside `step_instruction`,
which gets called over and over until it says the field is done.

Stop on this for a moment, because it is a slightly unusual shape next to
the "normal" emulator main loop that most references show you. The
conventional structure is two nested `for` loops:
`for line in 0..262 { for cycle in ... { cpu.step() } ; end_of_line() }`.
That version is easier to read cold and it computes exactly the same
thing. The comment above tells you this codebase used to be written that
way and was deliberately refactored into a single-instruction-granularity
primitive. The rest of this section is about why that refactor was worth
doing, because the reason is not obvious and it is not performance.

### Why hoist the loop counters into fields?

Look at what moved out of the old nested loops and into `Machine` itself,
at
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs:109-127`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs#L109-L127).
The doc comments are quoted in full because each one earns its keep — they
document not just what the field is but which loop variable it used to be:

```rust
/// Current scanline within the field, in `0..lines_per_field`. Was the
/// `for line in 0..lines` counter local to the old `run_field`; hoisting it
/// (with `line_cycles_spent`/`line_budget`) into the machine is what makes
/// execution resumable at instruction granularity. 0 at construction and
/// after every completed field.
line: u32,
/// CPU cycles executed in the current scanline so far. Was `spent` inside
/// the old `run_cycles`. Reset to 0 at each scanline boundary; the
/// invariant after any completed `step_instruction` is
/// `line_cycles_spent < line_budget` (mid-line) or `== 0` (just crossed
/// into a new line/field).
line_cycles_spent: u32,
/// This scanline's cycle budget, sampled once at the line's start (when
/// `line_cycles_spent == 0`) exactly like the old `run_field` sampled
/// `cycles_per_field()/lines` at the top of each loop iteration — so a
/// mid-field speed poke only takes effect on the next line. Also the
/// multiplier the end-of-line GIME timer tick uses. Meaningful only once a
/// line has begun; initialized to 0.
line_budget: u32,
```

The argument for the refactor is a single observation about scope. A `for`
loop's counter dies the moment the loop returns. If `run_field` were still
two nested `for` loops, the finest granularity at which you could ever stop
and later resume the machine would be "one whole field," because that is
the only point at which no loop variable is live. You could not single-step
one instruction, hand control back to a caller, and come back later without
re-deriving where in the field you had been — and re-deriving it is not
possible, because the information was on a stack frame that no longer
exists.

Promoting `line`, `line_cycles_spent`, and `line_budget` from stack-local
variables to fields on `Machine` changes that completely. The *entire run
state* becomes part of the machine's persistent state, which means the
machine is a valid, resumable machine at every instruction boundary rather
than only at field boundaries. That is exactly what a debugger needs, and
it is the reason all three fields exist.

Chapter 16's single-instruction step button, its "run until this scanline"
breakpoint, and its ability to pause mid-field and inspect registers
without corrupting the timing model are all the same thing: `run_field`
with a different stopping condition. You are looking at the substrate a
debugger gets built on, ten weeks before you build the debugger — and, as
the last part of this section shows, well after someone already built one
on it.

### `step_instruction`, in full

That hoisted state needs a consumer, and `step_instruction` is it. The doc
comment states the contract, at
[`run.rs:29-41`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L29-L41):

```rust
/// Execute exactly one instruction (or one burned HALT* cycle) with full
/// fidelity — peripheral ticks, NMI/FIRQ/IRQ servicing, and, when this step
/// crosses the current scanline's cycle budget, the per-line trailer
/// (hsync, the two field-sync edges at their lines, one audio sample, the
/// GIME timer tick) and the field wrap (render + `field_complete`). This is
/// the single primitive `run_field` and the debugger's `run_until` are both
/// built on; the two together reproduce the old `run_field`/`run_cycles`
/// nested loops one step at a time.
///
/// Ordering is load-bearing and preserved exactly from the old code (see
/// [`Machine::step_cpu_unit`] and [`Machine::end_of_line`]): the per-line
/// trailer runs immediately after the instruction that pushes the line over
/// budget, in the same call, before any instruction of the next line.
```

"Ordering is load-bearing" is the sentence to carry into the code. It is
not a general plea for care; it is a specific claim that the per-line
trailer must run before any instruction of the next line, and §6.5 explains
what breaks when it doesn't. Here is the function itself, at
[`run.rs:42-84`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L42-L84):

```rust
pub fn step_instruction(&mut self) -> StepEvent {
    let lines = self.config.video.lines_per_field();
    loop {
        // Sample this line's budget once, at its start — same point the old
        // `run_field` sampled `cycles_per_field()/lines`.
        if self.line_cycles_spent == 0 {
            self.line_budget = self.cycles_per_field() / lines;
        }
        // Mirror `run_cycles`' `while spent < budget`: run one CPU unit if
        // the line still has budget. For real video timing `line_budget` is
        // always well above one instruction, so this branch always runs;
        // the `else` only guards a degenerate zero-budget line.
        if self.line_cycles_spent < self.line_budget {
            let (cycles, was_instruction) = self.step_cpu_unit();
            self.line_cycles_spent += cycles;
            let kind = if was_instruction {
                StepKind::Instruction { cycles }
            } else {
                StepKind::HaltCycle
            };
            // `run_cycles` exits when spent >= budget; `run_field` then runs
            // the per-line trailer. A single CPU unit (≤ ~20 cycles) can
            // cross at most one ~57-cycle line boundary, so one trailer
            // suffices.
            let field_complete = if self.line_cycles_spent >= self.line_budget {
                let done = self.end_of_line();
                self.line_cycles_spent = 0;
                done
            } else {
                false
            };
            return StepEvent { kind, field_complete };
        }
        // Degenerate zero-budget line (never reached for real timing): no
        // CPU unit to run — do the trailer and continue to the next line so
        // every call still makes forward progress.
        let field_complete = self.end_of_line();
        self.line_cycles_spent = 0;
        if field_complete {
            return StepEvent { kind: StepKind::HaltCycle, field_complete: true };
        }
    }
}
```

Trace one call through it, slowly, because every later section refers back
to this control flow.

The first thing that happens is the budget check. `line_cycles_spent == 0`
is true on the very first call after construction and true again
immediately after any `end_of_line` resets it, so the budget gets sampled
at the start of every line and only at the start of every line. This is the
one and only place `cycles_per_field()` is ever consulted, and it is
consulted fresh each line — which is precisely why a mid-field
`POKE 65497,0` takes effect on the *next* line rather than retroactively
re-scoring the line already in progress. Sampling once per line is not a
performance optimization. It is a fidelity decision about when a speed
change becomes visible, and the doc comment on `line_budget` says so.

Then one "CPU unit" runs. §6.4 is entirely about what a unit is, but for
now it is either one retired instruction or one burned HALT cycle, and it
reports back how many cycles it consumed. That cost gets added to
`line_cycles_spent`, and the `was_instruction` flag chooses which
`StepKind` to report.

Now the interesting branch. If that unit pushed the line over its budget,
`end_of_line()` runs *immediately, in the same call*, before
`step_instruction` returns anything to its caller. This is the ordering the
doc comment called load-bearing. The per-line trailer must run before any
instruction of the next line gets to execute, because that trailer is what
raises the very interrupts the next instruction might need to see. Defer it
by even one instruction and a field-sync IRQ arrives one instruction late,
every field, forever — a small enough error to go unnoticed and a large
enough one to matter to software that counts.

The comment on that branch also answers a question a careful reader will
have already formed: what if one instruction is longer than a whole line?
It can't be. A single CPU unit costs at most about twenty cycles, and a
line's budget is 56 or more, so a unit can cross at most one line boundary
and one trailer therefore suffices. If the CoCo had a 200-cycle instruction
this code would need a loop here instead of an `if`.

The final block, after the `if`, is the degenerate case: a line whose
budget is zero, which real video timing never produces. It exists so that
every call to `step_instruction` makes forward progress no matter what, and
so that a caller can never construct a configuration that spins forever
inside one call. Guarding an unreachable case is cheap; discovering that
your debugger hangs because someone set a field rate of zero is not.

### `StepEvent`/`StepKind`: what one call reports

One call to `step_instruction` has two independent things to report, and
the return type says so. From
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs:137-164`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine.rs#L137-L164):

```rust
/// What a single [`Machine::step_instruction`] advanced. Both fields are
/// reported because one call can retire an instruction (or burn a HALT* cycle)
/// AND cross a scanline/field boundary in the same step — the debugger's
/// `run_until` needs the CPU action for its trace ring and the field flag for
/// its stop condition.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub struct StepEvent {
    /// What the CPU did this step.
    pub kind: StepKind,
    /// True when this step completed a video field: the per-scanline trailer
    /// for the field's last line ran, `line` wrapped back to 0, and the
    /// framebuffer was rendered — exactly the point the old `run_field`
    /// returned.
    pub field_complete: bool,
}

/// The CPU action a [`Machine::step_instruction`] performed.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum StepKind {
    /// One instruction retired, consuming `cycles` bus cycles.
    Instruction {
        /// Bus cycles the instruction consumed.
        cycles: u32,
    },
    /// One HALT* cycle was burned (the cartridge holds the bus low); the CPU
    /// did not advance and no instruction retired.
    HaltCycle,
}
```

The two fields are genuinely orthogonal, which is why they are two fields.
What the CPU did and whether a field boundary was crossed are independent
outcomes: a single call can retire an instruction and complete a field at
the same time, and the two consumers of this type want different halves of
it. `run_field` reads only `field_complete`. The debugger reads both —
`kind` to decide whether to push a trace entry, `field_complete` to decide
whether to stop.

> **Rust corner: an enum with payload beats two bools.** A more
> C-flavoured design would give `step_instruction` two output parameters:
> `was_halt_cycle: bool` and `cycles: u32` (meaningless when the bool is
> true), plus a third `field_complete: bool`. That's three loosely related
> facts, one of which is sometimes garbage, glued together by convention
> instead of the type system. `StepKind` makes the illegal state
> unrepresentable: you cannot construct a `StepKind::HaltCycle { cycles }`
> — the variant simply has no such field — so a caller matching on `kind`
> can never accidentally read a cycle count that doesn't apply. This is
> the same idea as `Option`/`Result`, familiar from any Rust codebase, just
> applied to a domain-specific pair of outcomes instead of a generic library
> type.
>
> `#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]` gives both types value
> semantics and comparability for free — no method bodies to write, and
> notice `Copy`: these are small enough (`StepKind` is at most a `u32` plus
> a discriminant) that cloning is cheaper than borrowing, so the derive
> lets callers use them like `i32`s without a second thought.

### Resumability's payoff: a live look at `Debugger::run_until`

Chapter 16's chapter is titled "the payoff of every earlier decision," but one
piece of that payoff already exists in this codebase today. It is worth
detouring into now, while `step_instruction`'s shape is fresh, rather than
taking on faith that "a debugger gets built on top of this." Open
[`crates/coco-core/src/debug.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/debug.rs). Its file header states the dependency
outright:

```rust
//! Debug core: the [`Debugger`] the frontend owns and drives, plus the
//! side-effect-free primitives the machine exposes for it (`docs/plan-debugger.md`
//! §2). The [`Debugger`] holds PC breakpoints and memory watchpoints, runs the
//! machine one instruction at a time via [`Machine::step_instruction`] until a
//! stop condition trips ([`Debugger::run_until`]), and keeps an instruction
//! trace ring for "how did I get here" / MAME trace-diffing.
```

"Runs the machine one instruction at a time via `Machine::step_instruction`"
— that's not a metaphor; it's the literal implementation. `StopReason`
enumerates why a run stopped ([`debug.rs:83-101`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/debug.rs#L83-L101)):

```rust
/// Why [`Debugger::run_until`] stopped.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq, Debug)]
pub enum StopReason {
    /// The PC reached an enabled breakpoint (before executing that
    /// instruction).
    Breakpoint(u16),
    /// A memory watchpoint tripped mid-instruction.
    Watchpoint {
        /// The watched logical address that was accessed.
        addr: u16,
        /// Read vs write.
        kind: WatchKind,
    },
    /// A video field completed (the machine reached a field boundary).
    FieldComplete,
    /// The instruction budget was exhausted without any of the above — the
    /// run made progress but hit no stop condition.
    Step,
}
```

Notice `FieldComplete` sitting right there next to `Breakpoint` and
`Watchpoint` — the debugger can stop a run exactly where `run_field` would
have stopped it anyway, because both are reading the same `field_complete`
flag off the same `StepEvent`. A user-facing debugger feature and the
emulator's own main loop are, at this level, the same condition with two
names. And the run loop itself
([`debug.rs:364-407`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/debug.rs#L364-L407), lightly trimmed to the control flow):

```rust
pub fn run_until(&mut self, m: &mut Machine, max_instructions: u64) -> StopReason {
    m.bus.install_watches(self.watch_table());
    let reason = self.run_loop(m, max_instructions);
    m.bus.uninstall_watches();
    reason
}

fn run_loop(&mut self, m: &mut Machine, max_instructions: u64) -> StopReason {
    for i in 0..max_instructions {
        let pc = m.cpu.pc;
        let stop_at_bp = i > 0 && matches!(self.breakpoints.get(&pc), Some(bp) if bp.enabled);
        if stop_at_bp {
            let bp = self.breakpoints.get_mut(&pc).expect("just matched above");
            bp.hits += 1;
            return StopReason::Breakpoint(pc);
        }

        m.bus.clear_watch_hit();
        let snapshot = self.trace_enabled.then(|| TraceEntry::capture(&m.cpu));
        let event = m.step_instruction();
        if let (true, Some(entry)) =
            (matches!(event.kind, StepKind::Instruction { .. }), snapshot)
        {
            self.push_trace(entry);
        }

        if let Some(hit) = m.bus.take_watch_hit() {
            if let Some(wp) = self.watchpoints.get_mut(&hit.addr) {
                wp.hits += 1;
            }
            return StopReason::Watchpoint {
                addr: hit.addr,
                kind: hit.kind,
            };
        }
        if event.field_complete {
            return StopReason::FieldComplete;
        }
    }
    StopReason::Step
}
```

There is no second CPU loop, no shadow interpreter for "debug mode" versus
"run mode" — `run_loop` is `for i in 0..max_instructions { ...
m.step_instruction() ... }` with three early-exit checks bolted around the
one call this whole chapter has been building up to. This is the entire
reason this section made a point of hoisting `line`/`line_cycles_spent`/
`line_budget` onto `Machine` instead of leaving them as loop-local
variables: a debugger that pauses after any single instruction needs the
machine to *still be a valid, resumable machine* the moment it stops, and
a resumable `step_instruction` is exactly what makes `m.step_instruction()`
safe to call once, inspect, then call again, forever, from any external
loop — the debugger's or yours.

**Proving it, not just reading it.** Two real, verified surprises turned
up while confirming this section against the actual code — both worth
knowing before you build on `run_until` in Chapter 16, and both good examples
of why this course tells you to run things rather than trust prose. Take a
tiny hand-assembled program — the same 13-byte "arm a field-sync IRQ, then
spin" pattern §6.7 builds out in full — with a breakpoint set at `$8100`,
the address the IRQ vector points at:

```rust
let mut dbg = Debugger::new();
dbg.trace_enabled = true;
dbg.add_breakpoint(0x8100); // the IRQ handler's entry point
let reason = dbg.run_until(&mut m, 1_000_000);
```

Run that, and `reason` comes back `FieldComplete` — the breakpoint on the
handler's entry address **never fires**, even though the CPU visibly runs
that handler every field. Move the breakpoint one instruction later, to
`$8103` (the handler's second instruction), and it fires immediately:
`Breakpoint(0x8103)`, hit count 1, at scanline 245. The reason is exactly
`run_loop`'s structure: it samples `pc = m.cpu.pc` *before* calling
`step_instruction`, but interrupt recognition happens *inside*
`step_instruction` — `step_cpu_unit` (§6.4) redirects `cpu.pc` to the
vector target and then immediately executes that first instruction, all
within the one call. The CPU's program counter genuinely is `$8100` for a
moment, mid-call — but that moment is never externally observable as "the
value of `m.cpu.pc` right before a `step_instruction` call," which is the
only moment `run_loop` ever checks. **A breakpoint on an interrupt vector's
target address is unreachable through this exact mechanism**, a real,
verified gap between "where interrupts logically transfer control" and
"where this debugger's breakpoint check happens to sample the PC." (If you
build Chapter 16's debugger UI, this is worth a code comment of its own —
and a candidate fix, e.g. checking breakpoints against the *new* PC
immediately after `service_interrupts` redirects it, is exactly the kind
of small, real, load-bearing decision Chapter 16 will ask you to make.)

The second surprise is quieter. With `trace_enabled` on, the trace ring
entry recorded for the very call that services the interrupt logs the PC
*as sampled before that call* — the spin loop's address, not the handler's
— because `TraceEntry::capture` runs before `step_instruction`, matching
`run_loop`'s own ordering. The trace ring is an honest record of "what PC
was about to execute when the loop asked," not "what instruction actually
retired this step" — a distinction that never matters for an ordinary
instruction (the two coincide) but does for the one step per interrupt
where they don't. Neither surprise is a bug to fix right now; both are
exactly what the documented ordering promises once you trace it through.
But neither is discoverable from reading `run_loop` alone, without actually
running it against a program that fires an interrupt — which is the whole
reason this subsection built one instead of describing `run_until` in the
abstract.

That is the outer half of the loop. What a "CPU unit" actually consists of
— and the hardware priority order buried in it — is the next section.

## 6.4 `step_cpu_unit`: HALT, then interrupts, then the CPU, then peripherals

`step_instruction` delegates the actual work of one step to a single
private helper. It is thirty-eight lines including its comments, and about
half of those comments describe a specific data-loss bug that the ordering
in this function exists to prevent. That ratio is a fair warning: this is
the densest function in the chapter, and every branch in it corresponds to
a pin on a real chip.

Here it is in full, from
[`run.rs:86-123`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L86-L123):

```rust
/// One iteration of the old `run_cycles` inner loop: burn a HALT* cycle or
/// execute one instruction, then tick the per-cycle peripherals. Returns
/// `(cycles, was_instruction)`.
///
/// The cartridge HALT* line has priority over everything (MC6809 pin
/// behaviour): while a device holds it — the FD-502's sector-transfer
/// handshake — the CPU sits at an instruction boundary burning cycles and
/// pending interrupts wait. The cartridge is ticked either way so it can
/// pace the very work (DRQ cadence) that releases the line.
///
/// The MC6809 recognizes interrupts only at the *end* of an instruction, so
/// the first instruction after HALT* releases must execute before any
/// pending NMI/IRQ/FIRQ is serviced. Skipping this lets the completion NMI
/// of an FD-502 sector read preempt the DSKCON copy loop's `STB ,X+` that
/// stores the sector's final byte — dropping one byte per sector on load.
fn step_cpu_unit(&mut self) -> (u32, bool) {
    let (cycles, was_instruction) = if self.bus.halt_asserted() {
        self.prev_halted = true;
        (1, false)
    } else {
        // Coming straight out of HALT, run one instruction before
        // acknowledging interrupts (they stay pending for next loop).
        if !self.prev_halted {
            self.bus.poll_cart_interrupt();
            if self.bus.take_nmi() {
                self.cpu.nmi(&mut self.bus);
            }
            self.service_interrupts();
        }
        self.prev_halted = false;
        (self.cpu.step(&mut self.bus), true)
    };
    self.bus.cart.tick(cycles);
    self.bus.cassette.tick(cycles, self.bus.pia1.a.c2_output());
    self.bus.bitbanger.tick(cycles, self.bus.pia1_tx_mark());
    self.bus.cycle_clock = self.bus.cycle_clock.wrapping_add(u64::from(cycles));
    (cycles, was_instruction)
}
```

Read the branches top to bottom, because the order encodes real hardware
priority and not just code convenience. Stated as a sequence, one unit of
work does this:

1. Check HALT*. If a cartridge is holding it, burn one cycle and skip
   everything else.
2. Otherwise, unless the previous unit was halted, poll the cartridge's
   level interrupt, then NMI, then IRQ and FIRQ.
3. Execute one instruction.
4. Tick the per-cycle peripherals with however many cycles this unit
   consumed, whichever branch it took.

Three of those four steps have a hardware reason behind them that is worth
unpacking.

### HALT* comes first, because the pin does

`self.bus.halt_asserted()` asks whether the cartridge port is holding the
CPU's HALT* pin low. That question has a one-line answer, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs:28-38`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs#L28-L38):

```rust
/// True while the cartridge holds the CPU HALT* line low (the FD-502's
/// sector-transfer handshake). The only HALT source on a stock CoCo 3 is
/// the expansion port.
pub fn halt_asserted(&self) -> bool {
    self.cart.halt_asserted()
}

/// Consume a pending NMI edge (the FD-502 gates FDC INTRQ onto NMI).
pub fn take_nmi(&mut self) -> bool {
    self.cart.take_nmi()
}
```

Both of these are pure forwarding to the cartridge, and that is itself the
interesting fact: on a stock CoCo 3 the expansion port is the *only* source
of either signal. Nothing on the motherboard can halt the CPU. If the
machine is halted, something plugged into it did that, and on a real
1980s desk the something was almost always the FD-502 disk controller in
the middle of a sector transfer. Chapter 13 tells that story properly.

When HALT* is asserted, the CPU does not run at all. One cycle is burned —
the `(1, false)` return says "one cycle elapsed, no instruction retired" —
`prev_halted` is set, and control falls straight through to the peripheral
ticks at the bottom of the function. This matches the real 6809E's pin
behavior: HALT* outranks everything, including interrupts. A halted 6809
is not deciding to ignore an interrupt request; it has no bus with which to
fetch a vector, and interrupts therefore wait.

### `prev_halted`: one bit that prevents a one-byte bug

The `if !self.prev_halted` guard around the interrupt polling is the subtle
part of this function, and it is the part the doc comment spends the most
words on. The hardware rule is simple enough to state: the MC6809
recognizes interrupts only *between* instructions, never in the middle of
one. The consequence, in this specific situation, takes a moment longer.

Consider what happens the instant HALT* releases. The CPU was parked at an
instruction boundary the whole time it was halted, and while it was parked
the device that halted it very likely raised an interrupt — that is, after
all, how the FD-502 announces that the sector it was transferring is
finished. So at the moment of release there is a pending NMI *and* a CPU
sitting at an instruction boundary, which looks exactly like the condition
for servicing that NMI immediately.

It isn't. On real hardware, the release of HALT* is followed by the CPU
resuming the instruction stream, and the interrupt is recognized at the end
of the *next* instruction. Get that backwards in an emulator and you
reproduce a real, specific, and thoroughly nasty bug, which the doc comment
names: the FD-502's end-of-sector NMI preempts the disk driver's final
`STB ,X+`, the instruction that stores the sector's last byte. Every sector
loaded from disk arrives one byte short. Files load. Programs mostly run.
And then something, somewhere, is wrong in a way that takes a day to trace
back to a missing byte at the end of every 256-byte block.

`prev_halted` is exactly one bit of memory carried across calls to enforce
"run one instruction before re-arming interrupt recognition." It costs a
`bool` on the `Machine` struct and it converts an entire class of disk
corruption into a non-event. This is a preview of Chapter 13's HALT/NMI
handshake, but notice that the ordering decision is made *here*, in week
6's file, in the general-purpose run loop, months before any disk
controller exists to care about it.

### The CPU, and then the peripherals no matter what

`self.cpu.step(&mut self.bus)` is the actual instruction — the thing weeks
2 through 4 built, called through the seam Chapter 1 designed, returning the
cycle count Chapter 2 copied out of a data sheet. It is one line in the middle
of this function and it is the only line that does anything a 6809
programmer would recognize.

The four lines after the `if`/`else` run unconditionally, using whatever
`cycles` this unit consumed — one for a burned HALT cycle, the real
instruction cost otherwise. They advance the cartridge, the cassette
motor and FSK decoder (Chapter 12), the bit-banged serial line (Chapter 14), and
the machine-wide `cycle_clock` that Chapter 11's audio pipeline timestamps its
events against.

The unconditional part is not laziness, and the doc comment flags why: "The
cartridge is ticked either way so it can pace the very work (DRQ cadence)
that releases the line." Think about what would happen if the peripheral
ticks lived inside the `else` branch, running only when an instruction
executed. A halted machine would stop ticking the cartridge. The cartridge
would therefore never advance the sector transfer that is the reason it
asserted HALT* in the first place. Nothing would ever satisfy the condition
that releases the line, and the emulator would hang — not crash, not
misbehave, just quietly stop, with a CPU that is behaving perfectly
correctly by burning one cycle at a time forever. Deadlocks in emulators
tend to look like this: every component individually correct, and the loop
between them never closed.

> **Rust corner: `Rc<Cell<T>>` as a test observation seam.** The test that
> pins all of this down builds a fake cartridge, and it needs two things at
> once: the cartridge has to be *moved into* the machine, and the test has
> to keep watching it from outside. Chapter 1's rule — the machine state is a
> plain owned tree, no shared ownership — makes that a genuine problem,
> because once `insert_cartridge` takes ownership there is no handle left.
>
> [`crates/coco-core/tests/halt.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/halt.rs)
> solves it with the one tool Chapter 1 said is tolerated at the edges, and
> the resulting cartridge fits in a single `impl` block:
>
> ```rust
> impl Cartridge for HaltCart {
>     fn read(&mut self, _addr: u16) -> u8 {
>         coco_core::cart::IO_OPEN_BUS
>     }
>     fn write(&mut self, _addr: u16, _val: u8) {}
>     fn tick(&mut self, cycles: u32) {
>         let before = self.ticks.get();
>         self.ticks.set(before + cycles);
>         // The NMI edge fires at the moment the halt releases.
>         if before < self.halt_until && before + cycles >= self.halt_until {
>             self.nmi_pending.set(true);
>         }
>     }
>     fn halt_asserted(&self) -> bool {
>         (self.halt_from..self.halt_until).contains(&self.ticks.get())
>     }
>     fn take_nmi(&mut self) -> bool {
>         self.nmi_pending.replace(false)
>     }
> }
> ```
>
> `ticks` and `nmi_pending` are `Rc<Cell<u32>>` and `Rc<Cell<bool>>`, and
> the test holds a clone of each. `Rc` is the shared-ownership pointer;
> `Cell` is the interior-mutability box that lets a value be replaced
> through a shared reference. Together they give the test a live window
> into a device the machine now owns — which is why the test can assert
> `ticks.get() >= HALT_UNTIL_CYCLES - 1000`, proving the cartridge kept
> being ticked *while the CPU was halted*, the exact deadlock-avoidance
> property described above.
>
> Note `halt_asserted(&self) -> bool` taking a shared reference: that is
> why `Cell` is needed rather than a plain field. And note where this
> lives — in `tests/`, never in the machine state tree, so save states are
> unaffected. This is the "tolerated only where something forces your hand"
> clause from Chapter 1, and a test observing a moved value is exactly the
> kind of hand-forcing it was written for.

> **Rust corner: `pub(super)`.** `end_of_line` (next section) is declared
> `pub(super) fn end_of_line(&mut self) -> bool` — visible to `run.rs`'s
> parent module (`machine.rs`, which does `mod run;`) and everything else
> under it, but not to `coco-egui` or any other crate. This is Rust's
> answer to "protected, but only within one directory of the tree," and
> it's how `machine/` stays split into focused files (`run.rs`,
> `render.rs`, `audio.rs`, `video_mode.rs`, all `mod`-declared in
> `machine.rs`) without every cross-file helper becoming `pub` and part of
> the crate's public API by accident. Grep the module for `pub(super)` as
> you read it — it's a reliable signal of "this exists to be called from a
> sibling file in the same subsystem, not from outside it."

## 6.5 `end_of_line`: the per-scanline trailer

Everything so far has been about running the CPU. This section is about the
other half of a scanline: the handful of things that happen once per line
*because* a line ended, in a fixed order, 262 times per field. The trailer
is where the emulator stops being a CPU with a bus and starts being a
television set, a speaker, and an interval timer — and it is the single
function that most of the remaining chapters in this book plug into.

`step_instruction` calls it the instant a line's cycle budget is exhausted.
Here it is in full, at
[`run.rs:130-172`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L130-L172):

```rust
pub(super) fn end_of_line(&mut self) -> bool {
    let lines = self.config.video.lines_per_field();
    let fs_falling_line = self.config.video.fs_falling_line(self.config.variant);
    let fs_rising_line = self.config.video.fs_rising_line(self.config.variant);
    self.bus.hsync();
    if self.line == fs_falling_line {
        self.bus.fs_falling();
    }
    if self.line == fs_rising_line {
        self.bus.fs_rising();
    }
    self.render_scanline();
    // Render this line's audio to the oversampled stereo grid,
    // self-capping when nothing drains it.
    if self.audio_buffer.len() >= super::AUDIO_BUFFER_CAP {
        self.audio_buffer.clear();
    }
    self.flush_line_audio();
    // GIME interval timer: TINS=1 counts the fixed 3.58 MHz clock — 4 ticks
    // per normal-speed CPU cycle, 2 per double-speed cycle — TINS=0 counts
    // horizontal syncs (1 per line). No such timer exists on the plain-SAM
    // path (CoCo 1/2) — the GIME stays completely inert there
    // (`docs/coco12-plan.md` Phase 4). `line_budget` is this line's sampled
    // cycle count — the old loop's `cycles_per_line`.
    if self.config.variant == MachineVariant::Coco3 {
        let ticks = if self.bus.gime.timer_is_fast() {
            let per_cycle =
                FAST_TIMER_TICKS_PER_CPU_CYCLE / if self.bus.gime.cpu_fast { 2 } else { 1 };
            self.line_budget * per_cycle
        } else {
            1
        };
        self.bus.gime.tick_timer(ticks);
    }
    self.line += 1;
    if self.line >= lines {
        self.line = 0;
        self.render_field();
        true
    } else {
        false
    }
}
```

The overall shape is: sync, then video, then audio, then the timer, then
advance the line counter and possibly wrap the field. Take those in order.

Every scanline gets `hsync()`, unconditionally. Only two specific scanlines
out of the whole field get a field-sync call, and — unlike the naive "one
big vsync pulse at the very end of the field" the design sketch proposed —
the CoCo's field sync is genuinely two separate edges, falling and rising,
sitting in the middle of the field on real NTSC timing. Then come
`render_scanline` (Chapter 7), the audio flush (Chapter 11), the GIME's interval
timer (Chapter 8's blink, among others), and finally the counter increment
that either returns `false` for "keep going" or wraps the field, renders
it, and returns `true` — the signal `step_instruction` propagates all the
way out to `run_field`'s `while` condition.

### The horizontal sync pulse, in full

`hsync()` is the most frequently called function in the entire emulator:
262 times per field, sixty times a second, for as long as the machine runs.
Its doc comment is longer than its body, and the first paragraph explains a
fidelity decision that this chapter's own tests depend on, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs:64-75`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs#L64-L75):

```rust
/// Horizontal-sync line: the GIME HS pin idles high and pulses low for 16
/// of 228 pixel clocks at line end (~4.5 µs; MAME `mc6847.cpp`
/// `TIMER_HSYNC_OFF_TIME`=212/`ON_TIME`=228). Our per-line model has no
/// resolution below one scanline, so both the falling and rising edges
/// are emitted back-to-back here rather than timed within the line; each
/// still only latches PIA0 CA1 (control reg $FF01, port A) and PIA1 CB1
/// (see below) if it matches that side's selected edge
/// ([`crate::pia::cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH`]), so software polling either edge —
/// stock BASIC's falling-edge CRA or NitrOS-9's rising-edge one — still
/// gets exactly one flag per line. The GIME HBORD source is raised once
/// per line regardless of edge selection: GIME border sources are
/// hardwired falling-edge only, not selectable (Lomont).
```

This is Chapter 1's fidelity budget in miniature, argued in a comment. The
real pulse is about 4.5 microseconds wide and sits at a specific point
within the line. This emulator's model has no resolution finer than one
scanline, so it cannot place the pulse *within* a line — and rather than
pretend otherwise or add sub-scanline machinery nothing needs, it emits
both edges back to back at the line boundary. The justification is in the
last clause: software polling either edge still gets exactly one flag per
line, which is the only property any CoCo program actually depends on.
Climb this fidelity rung only if something breaks.

The body, at
[`sync.rs:86-111`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs#L86-L111),
does rather more than pulse a line:

```rust
pub fn hsync(&mut self) {
    // No GIME on the plain-SAM path (CoCo 1/2): the PIA0/PIA1 Cx1 pulses
    // below stay exactly as-is, but the GIME border/keyboard/cart-EI0
    // interrupt sources it would also raise here don't exist — the GIME
    // struct must stay completely inert on that path
    // (`docs/coco12-plan.md` Phase 4).
    let is_gime = self.variant == MachineVariant::Coco3;
    self.pia0.a.set_c1(false);
    if is_gime {
        self.gime.raise(gime::intr::HBORD);
    }
    self.pia0.a.set_c1(true);
    // Buttons are included: SEB warns joystick fire buttons always trip EI1.
    let line_low = self.pia0_pa_pins() & 0x7F != 0x7F;
    if is_gime && line_low && !self.kbd_line_low {
        self.gime.raise(gime::intr::EI1);
    }
    self.kbd_line_low = line_low;
    if self.cart.cart_line_ties_q() {
        self.pia1.b.set_c1(false);
        if is_gime {
            self.gime.raise(gime::intr::EI0);
        }
        self.pia1.b.set_c1(true);
    }
}
```

The `set_c1(false)` immediately followed by `set_c1(true)` on PIA0's port A
is the back-to-back edge pair the doc comment described, and §6.6's tests
exercise exactly that pair. Between them, on a CoCo 3 only, the GIME's
HBORD interrupt source is raised — once per line, regardless of which edge
the PIA has selected, because GIME border sources are hardwired to the
falling edge rather than being software-selectable.

The two blocks after that are the reason this function is longer than you
would expect. The scanline boundary is also the GIME's sampling point for
its keyboard interrupt source, EI1, which fires when a zero appears on any
of PIA0's PA0–PA6 row sense lines while a column is strobed; the
`!self.kbd_line_low` term makes it an edge rather than a level, so a held
key raises the source once instead of every line. And it is the sampling
point for the expansion port's CART* line: an auto-start game pak ties that
pin to the Q clock, so while one is inserted this pulses PIA1's CB1 and
raises EI0 every single scanline, which is more cadence than either
interrupt path needs to stay fed.

Three subsystems — video timing, keyboard, and cartridge — all sample here,
for the same reason the audio flush does a few lines later in
`end_of_line`: the scanline boundary is a moment the code is already
crossing, so hanging periodic work off it costs no separate timer and no
separate loop.

### Where the field-sync edges land

The two field-sync calls are guarded by comparisons against numbers that
come from `config.rs`, and those numbers have unusually careful provenance.
Here is the falling edge's definition and its full doc comment, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/config.rs:65-90`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/config.rs#L65-L90):

```rust
/// Physical scanline (0-based) where the field-sync line falls: the point
/// where PIA0 CB1 latches per its selected edge, and — on the GIME — the
/// VBORD source is raised (Lomont: "VBORD generated on falling edge of
/// VSYNC").
///
/// NTSC, [`MachineVariant::Coco3`]: MAME `gime.cpp`'s constructor derives
/// the falling edge as top border (25 lines) + active display (192
/// lines) + part of the bottom border (26 lines) + 1 = 244. This is
/// GIME-specific — the plain MC6847 (CoCo 1/2) falling edge is at line
/// 216 instead (`mc6847.cpp`/`gime.cpp` header comment;
/// `docs/coco12-plan.md`).
pub const fn fs_falling_line(self, variant: MachineVariant) -> u32 {
    match self {
        VideoStandard::NTSC => match variant {
            MachineVariant::Coco3 => 244,
            MachineVariant::Coco1 | MachineVariant::Coco2 => 216,
        },
        // UNVERIFIED: MAME's PAL timing offsets this edge by
        // `LINES_PADDING_TOP_PAL` (mc6847.cpp), which could not be pinned
        // down cleanly from the source. Keep the pre-fix behaviour (the
        // field-sync edges collapsed to the last scanline of the field)
        // rather than guess a line number. CoCo 1/2 + PAL is rejected by
        // [`MachineConfig::validate`] before this is ever consulted.
        VideoStandard::PAL => VideoStandard::PAL.lines_per_field() - 1,
    }
}
```

Line 244 is not a measurement. It is a sum — 25 lines of top border, 192
lines of active display, 26 lines of bottom border, plus one — reconstructed
from MAME's own GIME constructor. That is worth knowing when you read it,
because it tells you what would change the number: any correction to the
CoCo 3's border geometry moves this edge with it.

Both edges, both variants, in one table:

| Edge | NTSC, CoCo 3 | NTSC, CoCo 1/2 | Source |
|------|:---:|:---:|---|
| Falling (`fs_falling_line`) | **244** | **216** | MAME `gime.cpp` border-sum vs. `mc6847.cpp` directly |
| Rising (`fs_rising_line`) | **248** | **248** | MAME `mc6847.cpp` `LINES_UNTIL_RETRACE_NTSC` + `LINES_VERTICAL_RETRACE` − 1 |

Notice that the CoCo 3 and the CoCo 1/2 fall at *different* lines, 244
against 216. The GIME's own border geometry does not match the plain
MC6847's, and the code keeps both numbers rather than picking one and
hoping nothing notices. The rising edge, by contrast, is the same 248 for
every variant — and the reason is more interesting than "they're the same
chip," because they aren't. Its derivation cites the plain MC6847's timing
constants directly rather than any GIME-specific approximation, and the doc
comment on `fs_rising_line` is candid about what that means: the value is
shared "absent a source that shows the GIME diverging from the real chip on
this edge the way it does on the falling one." That is an honest "we
haven't found evidence it's different," not a confident "we've confirmed
it's the same," and the difference between those two statements is exactly
the kind of thing a comment should preserve.

PAL is handled more bluntly still. Both edges are marked `UNVERIFIED` and
collapsed to the last scanline of the field rather than guessed at. Since
`MachineConfig::validate` rejects the CoCo 1/2 plus PAL combination
outright, the PAL branch is only ever reached by a CoCo 3 — where the same
"not confirmed" caveat applies.

This is a good specimen of a theme that recurs throughout the course and
gets its own appendix. When the authoritative sources disagree or fall
silent, the honest move is a code comment that says so, plus a value chosen
to be obviously provisional. A silently confident wrong number is worse
than an admitted gap, because the gap can be closed by someone who reads
the comment and the wrong number can only be found by someone who
reproduces the bug.

### Why audio flushes once per line, not once per instruction or once per field

`flush_line_audio()` is Chapter 11's chapter in full, at
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine/audio.rs:41`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/audio.rs#L41).
What matters to *this* chapter is not its internals but its placement, and
placement is a real decision with three plausible answers.

Some background is needed to see why. The core does not sample the DAC
register on a fixed clock the way a naive design would. It records every
DAC and mux write as it happens, cycle-timestamped, into an event list on
`SystemBus::audio_events`, and `flush_line_audio` is the function that
replays those events into actual audio samples. Because the events already
carry exact timestamps, the flush can happen at any cadence without losing
information about *when* things happened — the cadence only controls how
finely the output grid is resolved.

Flushing once per instruction would be correct in principle and wasteful in
practice. Audio only needs to be sampled fast enough to reconstruct sound a
human ear can hear, and the emulator already targets four grid slots per
scanline, `audio::OVERSAMPLE = 4`, which works out to roughly 62.9 kHz on
NTSC — comfortably above the 20 kHz ceiling any speaker or ear cares about.
Rendering a new grid slot after every single 6809 instruction would
recompute that grid tens of times more often than its own output resolution
can distinguish. You would be doing arithmetic whose results are discarded
by the next arithmetic.

Flushing once per field would be too coarse in a way that is easy to
underestimate. A field is about 16.7 milliseconds, which is long enough for
a 1200 Hz cassette tone to complete twenty full cycles, or a few-hundred-hertz
music note to complete several. Quantizing an entire field's worth of DAC
events down to one shot would flatten every waveform inside it into noise.
That is exactly the "correct values, wrong time resolution" failure §6.1
opened this chapter with, applied to sound rather than to the CPU clock.

Once per line sits at about 15.7 kHz on NTSC, already faster than any
audible transition rate an 8-bit DAC or a cassette FSK tone produces, and
it is free bookkeeping in a specific sense. `end_of_line` already visits
every scanline boundary for the sync pulses, so hanging the audio flush off
the same trailer costs no separate timer and no separate loop — just one
more call at a boundary the code was already crossing. Each line then gets
oversampled four times internally, so the *output* grid rather than the
flush cadence is the roughly 62.9 kHz figure a resampler downstream can
work with.

Notice the self-capping check on the line right before the flush call. A
headless run — the roughly 1500-field boot test in §6.8, for instance —
never calls `Machine::take_audio()` to drain the buffer, and would
otherwise grow that `Vec` without bound over twenty-five seconds of
emulated audio. `AUDIO_BUFFER_CAP` is sized at `8 * 262 * OVERSAMPLE`
samples, about eight fields' worth, so a draining consumer such as the real
frontend never comes close to the cap, and a non-draining one such as this
chapter's tests never leaks. Two lines of defensive code, and the reason
they exist is that headless testing is a first-class use of this codebase
rather than an afterthought.

### The GIME timer: rate selection, a documentation drift worth catching, and Chapter 8's blink

The last piece of the trailer is the block guarded by
`if self.config.variant == MachineVariant::Coco3`, which feeds elapsed
cycles into the GIME's 12-bit interval timer. Two fields hold its state, at
[`crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs:208-212`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs#L208-L212):

```rust
/// 12-bit timer programmed value ($FF94 low nibble / $FF95). Zero inhibits
/// the count; nonzero reloads (+[`TIMER_RELOAD_OFFSET`]) on each underflow.
pub timer_reload: u16,
/// Live countdown, in timer input clocks (INIT1 TINS selects the rate).
pub timer_count: u16,
```

A programmed reload value and a live countdown. The function that advances
the countdown is short enough to read in one go, at
[`gime.rs:378-394`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs#L378-L394):

```rust
/// Advance the 12-bit timer by `ticks` input clocks. Each underflow raises
/// the TMR interrupt source, toggles the text blink phase, and reloads
/// (SEB Unravelled II; MAME `timer_elapsed`). Inhibited while the
/// programmed value is zero.
pub fn tick_timer(&mut self, ticks: u32) {
    if self.timer_reload == 0 {
        return;
    }
    let mut remaining = ticks;
    while remaining >= u32::from(self.timer_count) {
        remaining -= u32::from(self.timer_count);
        self.blink_state = !self.blink_state;
        self.raise(intr::TMR);
        self.restart_timer();
    }
    self.timer_count -= remaining as u16;
}
```

The `while` rather than an `if` is the detail to catch. `end_of_line` hands
this function a whole line's worth of ticks at once — which, as the next
paragraphs show, can be over two hundred — and a timer programmed with a
small reload value can therefore underflow several times inside a single
call. The loop drains the ticks one underflow at a time, raising the
interrupt source and toggling the blink phase on each, and whatever is left
over decrements the fresh count. A version written with `if` would silently
lose underflows at fast timer rates, and the software that noticed would be
music code.

Which of two clocks feeds the countdown is selected by INIT1's TINS bit at
`$FF91`, bit `0x20`. Here a small but genuine documentation drift is worth
pointing out, precisely because it is the kind of thing you would otherwise
only find by cross-reading two files that do not cite each other. The
bit-layout comment in `gime.rs` itself, at
[`crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs:62-68`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs#L62-L68),
still says this:

```rust
/// INIT1 ($FF91) bit assignments (SEB Unravelled II).
pub mod init1 {
    /// Timer input select: 1 = ~70 ns (14.318 MHz), 0 = ~63.5 µs (horizontal rate).
    pub const TINS: u8 = 0x20;
```

But the constant that actually converts that selection into tick counts,
back in `machine.rs` next door to §6.2's `CPU_HZ`, tells a different story:

```rust
/// GIME timer input clocks per normal-speed CPU cycle with INIT1 TINS=1. The
/// fast timer clock is 3.579545 MHz (279.365 ns — hardware-measured; MAME
/// `gime.cpp`. SEB's "70 ns" is wrong), exactly 4× the 0.89 MHz CPU clock —
/// and 2× the double-speed CPU clock, since the timer runs off the fixed
/// video crystal and ignores the CPU rate. With TINS=0 the input is the
/// ~63.5 µs horizontal sync: one tick per scanline.
const FAST_TIMER_TICKS_PER_CPU_CYCLE: u32 = 4;
```

Read those two comments side by side. `gime.rs` still quotes SEB Unravelled
II's "~70 ns (14.318 MHz)" for the fast clock. `machine.rs` says flatly
that "SEB's '70 ns' is wrong" and uses 279.365 ns instead — that is
3.579545 MHz, the NTSC color subcarrier itself, rather than the 14.318 MHz
dot clock four times faster than it — citing MAME's `gime.cpp` as the
correction.

The *behavior* is not in question. `FAST_TIMER_TICKS_PER_CPU_CYCLE = 4` is
what `end_of_line` actually multiplies by, and it encodes the corrected
figure: four ticks of a 3.58 MHz clock per one tick of the 0.895 MHz CPU
clock checks out, since `3.579545 / 0.894886 ≈ 4.0`. What went stale is the
doc comment on `init1::TINS`, which never got updated when the correction
landed elsewhere in the crate. The division by two when `cpu_fast` is set
follows from the same fact: the timer runs off the fixed video crystal and
ignores the CPU rate entirely, so doubling the CPU clock halves the number
of timer ticks per CPU cycle without changing the timer's actual rate at
all.

This is the same territory as the conflicting-sources problem the appendix
covers, except that this time the conflict is between two comments in the
*same* codebase rather than between two external references. Take it as a
live reminder that "grep every doc comment that touches a claim" beats
trusting the first one you find, even inside a crate you already trust.

Two more details are worth carrying forward, both narrow and both
load-bearing for later weeks.

The first is `TIMER_RELOAD_OFFSET`, at
[`gime.rs:162-165`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs#L162-L165).
The 12-bit timer counts the programmed value *plus two* extra ticks on
every reload, a fact `gime.rs` attributes to the 1986 GIME as measured by
MAME's `reset_timer`, noting that the 1987 revision uses plus one instead.
Two different silicon revisions of the same chip with two different
off-by-a-small-constant behaviors, and this emulator picked one — the 1986
chip — and says which in the comment rather than splitting the difference
or quietly guessing. If you ever need to emulate a specific real CoCo 3
board revision precisely, that is the exact line to revisit.

The second is `blink_state`, at
[`gime.rs:220-221`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/gime.rs#L220-L221),
a single `pub bool` toggled on every timer underflow by the `tick_timer`
loop above. That one boolean is where Chapter 8's blinking attribute text and
blinking cursor come from.
[`machine/render.rs:54`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/render.rs#L54)
reads `self.bus.gime.blink_state` into a local called `blink_on` and
threads it down through `gime_video::paint_scanline` into `paint_text_row`,
where a character whose `ATTR_BLINK` bit is set shows its glyph only when
that boolean is true — literally, half the time, whichever half the timer
currently says.

The point to take from that is one of framing. The GIME timer whose
tick-rate arithmetic you just read is not a video feature at all from this
chapter's vantage point. It is a general-purpose interval timer that week
8's renderer happens to be one consumer of, and Chapter 8 is not even its most
important consumer — any program that wants a periodic interrupt without
counting instructions uses it. `end_of_line` is simply where that timer
gets fed, once per line, regardless of who is listening for its interrupt
or reading its toggling boolean.

## 6.6 Interrupt plumbing: the wired-OR, and a debugging war story

The trailer raises sync pulses. This section follows one of those pulses
all the way to the CPU's interrupt vector, through seven calls and two
chips, and then tells the story of what happens when one link in that chain
is missing. It is the most important section in the chapter, because the
chain it describes is the difference between a machine that boots and a
machine that doesn't.

### The wire

Start with a wiring problem that predates the CoCo by a decade. Two chips
can each independently want the CPU's attention, and the 6809 has exactly
two maskable interrupt pins, IRQ and FIRQ, to receive that attention on.
Real hardware solves this the way you would solve it with two switches and
one light bulb: wire them in parallel, so either switch turns the bulb on
and the bulb has no way of knowing which one did. The industry name for
that arrangement is *wired-OR*, and on the CoCo 3 the two switches are
PIA0 and the GIME. Here is the emulator's version, at
[`crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs:11-26`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs#L11-L26):

```rust
/// True when any interrupt source is holding the CPU IRQ line low.
///
/// PIA0's output and the GIME's IRQ output are wired-OR on the CPU pin: at
/// the BASIC prompt the stock ROM runs the legacy PIA path (INIT0 IEN=0)
/// off PIA0's field/horizontal sync, while GIME-native software enables
/// IEN and the $FF92 sources instead (`DESIGN.md` §4).
pub fn irq_asserted(&self) -> bool {
    self.pia0.irq() || self.gime.irq_asserted()
}

/// True when any interrupt source is holding the CPU FIRQ line low: PIA1
/// (the legacy cartridge FIRQ path) wired-OR with the GIME's FIRQ output
/// ($FF93 sources gated by INIT0 FEN).
pub fn firq_asserted(&self) -> bool {
    self.pia1.irq() || self.gime.firq_asserted()
}
```

The `||` *is* the wired-OR, and the correspondence is exact in a way that
is worth pausing on. Rust's `||` short-circuits, so `gime.irq_asserted()`
is not even called once `pia0.irq()` has returned `true` — harmless here,
since neither call has a side effect worth preserving. More importantly,
the CPU never learns *which* source asserted the line, exactly as on real
silicon. An interrupt pin carries a level, not a labeled event. Working
out who pulled it low is the interrupt handler's job, which is why every
interrupt handler ever written for this machine begins by reading status
registers.

The PIA side of that expression bottoms out in two more one-liners. Each
side of an MC6821 asserts IRQ when its Cx1 flag is latched *and* its
enable bit is on, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs:87-90`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs#L87-L90):

```rust
/// True when this side is asserting IRQ (Cx1 flag set and its enable on).
fn irq(&self) -> bool {
    self.control & cr::C1_FLAG != 0 && self.control & cr::C1_IRQ_ENABLE != 0
}
```

and the chip as a whole ORs its two sides together, at
[`pia.rs:181-183`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs#L181-L183):

```rust
/// Combined IRQ output of both sides.
pub fn irq(&self) -> bool {
    self.a.irq() || self.b.irq()
}
```

Two `||`s stacked on one more `||`: port A or port B, PIA0 or the GIME.
Four potential sources collapsing into one boolean, which is precisely what
four wires soldered to one pin do.

`service_interrupts` is what actually delivers the result to the CPU, at
[`run.rs:174-183`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/run.rs#L174-L183),
called from `step_cpu_unit` on every unit that isn't coming out of a halt:

```rust
/// Deliver pending FIRQ/IRQ to the CPU. The CPU itself honours the F/I masks
/// and leaves a masked, still-asserted line pending for the next check.
fn service_interrupts(&mut self) {
    if self.bus.firq_asserted() {
        self.cpu.firq(&mut self.bus);
    }
    if self.bus.irq_asserted() {
        self.cpu.irq(&mut self.bus);
    }
}
```

`cpu.firq` and `cpu.irq` are Chapter 4's territory. They check the CPU's own F
and I mask bits in the condition-code register and do nothing at all if the
relevant mask is set, which leaves the bus line asserted for next time.
That division of labour is worth stating explicitly: the bus knows whether
a line is being *held*, the CPU knows whether it is currently *listening*,
and neither one needs to know the other's answer. `service_interrupts` just
re-checks the wired-OR at every instruction boundary and offers the CPU a
chance to act. FIRQ is offered first, because it is the faster and
higher-priority of the two.

### How one edge becomes a flag: `PIAPort::set_c1`

There is one piece still missing from the chain, and it is the piece that
does the actual conversion. Field sync is a *level* — a physical line that
goes low and later goes high again. What the CPU can poll and clear is a
*latched flag* in a register. Turning the first into the second is the
MC6821's job, and the CoCo's use of it depends on a detail that is easy to
skip: the flag latches on one edge direction only, and which direction is
software-selectable.

The relevant control bits have names in this codebase, at
[`crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs:16-34`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs#L16-L34).
Four of these eight bits show up repeatedly for the rest of the chapter, so
it is worth having the whole set in view:

```rust
/// Control-register bit assignments (identical for CRA and CRB).
pub mod cr {
    /// Cx1 interrupt enable — 1 = an active Cx1 edge asserts IRQ.
    pub const C1_IRQ_ENABLE: u8 = 0x01;
    /// Cx1 active-edge select — 0 = high→low, 1 = low→high.
    pub const C1_EDGE_HIGH: u8 = 0x02;
    /// Data/DDR access select — 1 = data register, 0 = data-direction register.
    pub const DDR_ACCESS: u8 = 0x04;
    /// Cx2 output level when bits 5:4 select set/reset output mode.
    pub const C2_SET: u8 = 0x08;
    /// Cx2 output-mode select — with [`C2_OUTPUT`], 1 = set/reset (static level
    /// from [`C2_SET`]), 0 = handshake/pulse strobes (unmodelled).
    pub const C2_SET_RESET: u8 = 0x10;
    /// Cx2 direction — 1 = Cx2 is an output pin.
    pub const C2_OUTPUT: u8 = 0x20;
    /// Cx2 interrupt flag (read-only). Modelled as storage only.
    pub const C2_FLAG: u8 = 0x40;
    /// Cx1 interrupt flag (read-only) — set by an active Cx1 edge.
    pub const C1_FLAG: u8 = 0x80;
}
```

Bit 0 enables the interrupt. Bit 1 chooses which edge counts. Bit 2 decides
whether reads and writes at the data address hit the data register or the
data direction register, which will matter enormously in §6.7. Bit 7 is
the latched flag itself, and it is read-only from the CPU's side — a
program cannot fake an interrupt by setting it.

With those names in hand, the conversion function reads cleanly. From
[`crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs:66-85`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs#L66-L85):

```rust
/// Drive the Cx1 line to `level`, latching the Cx1 flag only on a real
/// transition whose direction matches CRA/CRB bit 1 ([`cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH`]):
/// bit1=1 selects low→high, bit1=0 selects high→low. A call that repeats
/// the current level (no edge) never sets the flag, and an edge in the
/// non-selected direction doesn't either — matching MAME `6821pia.cpp`
/// `c1_low_to_high`/`c1_high_to_low` (~line 1107): the flag sets iff
/// `(m_in_c1 != state) && ((state && rising_selected) || (!state &&
/// falling_selected))`. The CoCo wires horizontal sync to PIA0 CA1 and
/// field sync to PIA0 CB1.
pub fn set_c1(&mut self, level: bool) {
    let transitioned = self.c1_level != level;
    self.c1_level = level;
    if !transitioned {
        return;
    }
    let rising_selected = self.control & cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH != 0;
    if level == rising_selected {
        self.control |= cr::C1_FLAG;
    }
}
```

Two gates, in order. The first is `transitioned`: a call that repeats the
level already present is not an edge and sets nothing, which is why the
port has to remember `c1_level` at all. The second is the direction check,
and the expression `level == rising_selected` is a compact way to write
"the edge went the way the control register asked for." If the rising edge
is selected and the level just went high, the flag latches; if the falling
edge is selected and the level just went low, likewise; the two mismatched
combinations do nothing.

The two field-sync entry points that drive it are shorter still — three
lines and six — from
[`sync.rs:113-133`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/bus/sync.rs#L113-L133):

```rust
/// Field-sync falling edge (~60/50 Hz vertical, at
/// [`crate::config::VideoStandard::fs_falling_line`] scanlines into the
/// field, not at end-of-field): latches PIA0 CB1 (control reg $FF03, port
/// B) — the interrupt that drives BASIC's housekeeping loop — per its
/// selected edge, and raises the GIME VBORD source (Lomont: "VBORD
/// generated on falling edge of VSYNC").
pub fn fs_falling(&mut self) {
    self.pia0.b.set_c1(false);
    // No GIME (hence no VBORD source) on the plain-SAM path — Phase 4.
    if self.variant == MachineVariant::Coco3 {
        self.gime.raise(gime::intr::VBORD);
    }
}

/// Field-sync rising edge, at
/// [`crate::config::VideoStandard::fs_rising_line`] scanlines into the
/// field: latches PIA0 CB1 per its selected edge (e.g. NitrOS-9-style
/// rising-edge polling). No GIME border source is tied to this edge.
pub fn fs_rising(&mut self) {
    self.pia0.b.set_c1(true);
}
```

Note the asymmetry, because exercise 6.6 asks you to account for it. The
falling edge does two things — latches PIA0's CB1 and raises the GIME's
VBORD interrupt source. The rising edge does exactly one, because no GIME
border source is tied to it at all. The GIME's own interrupt block has an
opinion about which edge of vertical sync matters, and the PIA does not.

That completes the chain. When `end_of_line` reaches line 244 on a CoCo 3
running NTSC, it calls `bus.fs_falling()`, which calls
`pia0.b.set_c1(false)`. If PIA0's control register B has the falling edge
selected — which is the stock ROM's default, `cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH` clear —
the Cx1 flag latches. `PIAPort::irq()` now reports `true`, so
`MC6821::irq()` does, so `bus.irq_asserted()` does, so the next call to
`service_interrupts` offers the CPU an IRQ, and `cpu.irq(&mut bus)` takes
it. The flag stays latched until something *reads* PIA0's port B data
register, which clears it as a side effect of the read — the very fact that
forced `Bus::read` to take `&mut self` back in Chapter 1, coming due five
weeks later exactly as advertised.

Written as a chain, it is seven calls deep: `end_of_line` →
`bus.fs_falling()` → `pia0.b.set_c1(false)` → `pia0.irq()` →
`bus.irq_asserted()` → `service_interrupts` → `cpu.irq(&mut bus)`. Past
`end_of_line` itself, every link in that chain is under ten lines of
code. What follows is what happens
when one of them isn't there at all.

### The war story

Here's the discovery [`DESIGN.md`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/DESIGN.md) §4 documents under a dated implementation
note, and it's worth telling as what it actually was: a debugging session
where an emulator that was doing everything *correctly* still produced a
machine that looked *dead*.

> **Implementation note (2026-07, verified against the real ROM):** the
> stock CoCo 3 sits at the BASIC prompt with **INIT0 IEN=0/FEN=0**, i.e.
> the *legacy PIA path*, not the GIME interrupt block. The 60 Hz field
> sync is wired to **PIA0 CB1** (`$FF03`) and horizontal sync to **PIA0
> CA1** (`$FF01`); both drive IRQ. That field-sync IRQ is what breaks the
> ROM out of its `BRA *` idle loop.

Picture building this emulator in the natural order. The CPU comes first
and it is correct, because Chapters 2 through 4 proved it against hand-traced
flags and cycle counts. The bus and memory decode come second and they are
correct too, because Chapter 5's `bus_map.rs` tests all pass. Third comes the
shiny new GIME's own interrupt controller — because that is the
*interesting* chip, the headline part, the custom silicon that defines the
CoCo 3. Starting with the GIME is exactly what this very course's founding
request in `CLAUDE.md` proposes, and it is a completely reasonable plan.

So you wire the GIME's `$FF92` and `$FF93` interrupt-enable registers
correctly. You boot real Color BASIC. The CPU executes millions of
instructions — you can single-step it and watch registers change exactly as
documented, cycle for cycle right by every measure you have. And the screen
just sits there. No cursor blink, no sign-on banner update, no input
accepted, no sign of life at all.

That is a uniquely disorienting bug, and it is worth naming why. Every
diagnostic instinct you have points at the CPU, because the CPU is the part
that does things. But the obvious question — "is the CPU broken?" — has an
unambiguous, frustrating "no." Every fact about the CPU is correct and the
*machine* is nonetheless frozen. There is nothing to bisect, because
nothing is wrong.

The resolution is the note above. BASIC's idle loop is a literal `BRA *` —
branch to self, spin forever — and it is *designed* to be interrupted. It
does not poll a flag in a loop waiting for something to change. It sits
doing nothing at all and trusts an interrupt handler to do the housekeeping
— blink the cursor, scan the keyboard — and `RTI` back into the spin. The
loop is not waiting for work; it is waiting to be *preempted*.

On real hardware, and in the ROM's actual power-on configuration, that
interrupt is not routed through the fancy new GIME interrupt block at all.
`$FF92`'s IEN bit is off at the BASIC prompt. The interrupt is routed
through the boring, decades-older MC6821 PIA0, exactly the way a plain
CoCo 1 would do it, because Tandy's ROM authors kept the legacy path as the
CoCo 3's power-on default for compatibility with everything that came
before. Skip modeling PIA0's field-sync IRQ — a perfectly reasonable thing
to skip, if you are following "GIME first" as the obvious build order — and
you get a simulator that is instruction-perfect and functionally comatose.

This is exactly why the reading order in this course puts the bus and the
humble PIA, Chapter 10 in depth, ahead of the GIME's native video and
interrupt block in Chapters 7 through 9. The thing that actually makes BASIC
alive is not the glamorous chip. It rarely is.

### Proof, not assertion: walking `pia_sync.rs`

Everything this section has claimed about edge gating and scanline
placement is backed by a test file that needs no ROM at all —
[`crates/coco-core/tests/pia_sync.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/pia_sync.rs) builds a bare `SystemBus` and drives
`hsync`/`fs_falling`/`fs_rising` directly. Its own header states exactly
what it's proving:

```rust
//! PIA0 Cx1 edge-gating fidelity: CA1/CB1 flags must only latch on the
//! control-register-selected edge (`pia::cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH`), and the field
//! sync (CB1/VBORD) must land at its real mid-field scanlines
//! (`config::VideoStandard::fs_falling_line`/`fs_rising_line`), not at the
//! end of the field.
```

Two claims, two pairs of tests. Edge selection comes first, at
[`pia_sync.rs:26-41`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/pia_sync.rs#L26-L41):

```rust
#[test]
fn falling_edge_selected_port_flags_only_on_high_to_low() {
    let mut b = bus();
    // Default CRA ($FF01=0): C1_EDGE_HIGH clear -> falling edge selected.
    assert_eq!(b.pia0.a.control & cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH, 0);
    b.hsync(); // emits set_c1(false) then set_c1(true): falling edge matches
    assert_ne!(b.pia0.a.control & cr::C1_FLAG, 0);
}

#[test]
fn rising_edge_selected_port_flags_only_on_low_to_high() {
    let mut b = bus();
    b.write(PIA0_CRA, cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH); // select low->high
    b.hsync(); // falling edge (no match) then rising edge (matches)
    assert_ne!(b.pia0.a.control & cr::C1_FLAG, 0);
}
```

Both tests exercise `hsync()` rather than either field-sync function, which
looks odd until you remember the body quoted back in §6.5: `hsync()` calls
`self.pia0.a.set_c1(false)` immediately followed by `set_c1(true)` on every
single line, because the emulator has no sub-scanline resolution in which
to place the real pulse's two edges separately.

That back-to-back pair is exactly what makes both tests possible from one
function call. Whichever edge direction the control register selects, one
of the two `set_c1` calls inside a single `hsync()` will match it — and
`set_c1`'s direction check guarantees the other one, being mismatched, sets
nothing. Flip `cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH` between the two tests and you are really
just flipping which of `hsync`'s two calls is the one that counts. One
function call, two mutually exclusive outcomes, and the control register
picks between them.

Scanline placement comes second, in the pair that pins down the exact
numbers from §6.5's table. Here is the falling-edge half, at
[`pia_sync.rs:50-70`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/pia_sync.rs#L50-L70);
its `fs_rising` sibling is the mirror image against `fs_rising_line`:

```rust
#[test]
fn cb1_falling_flag_first_appears_at_fs_falling_line_not_before() {
    let mut b = bus();
    // Default CRB ($FF03=0): falling edge selected, matching stock BASIC's
    // $34/$35 ROM setup.
    let falling_line = VideoStandard::NTSC.fs_falling_line(MachineVariant::Coco3);
    for _ in 0..falling_line {
        b.hsync(); // drives CA1 only; CB1 must stay untouched all field
        assert_eq!(
            b.pia0.b.control & cr::C1_FLAG,
            0,
            "CB1 flag must not appear before the field-sync falling edge"
        );
    }
    b.fs_falling();
    assert_ne!(
        b.pia0.b.control & cr::C1_FLAG,
        0,
        "CB1 flag must appear exactly at the falling-edge scanline"
    );
}
```

Notice the shape of it, because the shape is the point. The test does not
merely assert that the flag is set after `fs_falling()`. It drives
`hsync()` exactly `falling_line` times first — 244, obtained from the same
`fs_falling_line` function §6.5 introduced rather than hard-coded — and
asserts *inside the loop, on every single iteration*, that CB1's flag is
still clear.

That loop is a test proving a negative: nothing before line 244 ever
touches CB1. It is a stronger and considerably more useful claim than "line
244 sets it," because the negative half is what a sloppy refactor would
actually violate. Accidentally call `fs_falling()` from inside `hsync()`
and the positive assertion still passes cheerfully; the loop catches it on
the first iteration.

The companion `cb1_rising_edge_...` test does the analogous thing for the
rising edge with an extra twist. It deliberately calls `fs_falling()` at
its normal line, mid-loop, specifically to prove that firing the
wrong-direction edge for this port's control-register setting — the test
selects `cr::C1_EDGE_HIGH`, so rising — still doesn't flag. That is the
same "wrong direction never counts" guarantee from the edge-selection pair,
now exercised at the real field-sync scanline instead of at every hsync.

Between these four tests and the `PIAPort::set_c1` code they exercise, this
section's whole account of "field sync becomes an IRQ" has been demonstrated
rather than asserted — which is exactly the property exercise 6.2 below
asks you to break on purpose and watch fail.

## 6.7 A synthetic boot, traced: reset vector to first field-sync IRQ

§6.8 is the real thing — real Color BASIC, a real sign-on banner, a real
`PRINT 2+2`. But that lab needs `roms/bas12.rom`, which a fresh checkout
genuinely doesn't have, any more than it has the `docs/` reference PDFs
`CLAUDE.md` mentions: both are git-ignored, machine-local, copyrighted
resources, and neither ships with the repository. Rather than describe a
real ROM's cold-start sequence from memory, or from a disassembly that
cannot be verified without those files, this section builds the *smallest
program that exercises the exact mechanism §6.6 just proved* — arm a
field-sync IRQ, idle in a spin loop, get woken up once per field — and
actually runs it, so every number below is a real `cargo test` result, not
a prediction.

It's not Color BASIC. It is, in miniature, the identical hardware
conversation Color BASIC has with PIA0 every field: these are the
load-bearing thirteen bytes underneath the `BRA *` idle loop §6.6's war
story described in the abstract. The point of building it is that the
mechanism is small enough to hold entirely in your head, which means every
number it produces can be predicted, checked, and — when the prediction is
wrong — explained.

### The program

Eight instructions, split across a cold-start preamble and an interrupt
handler — written the same way [`tests/halt.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/halt.rs) and [`tests/speed.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/speed.rs) build
their own synthetic ROMs (§6.9's reading list), reusing several of their
*exact* byte sequences (`LDS #$5EFF`, `BRA *`, `INC $0400`, `RTI` — all
independently verified there already):

```
$8000  10 CE 5E FF   LDS  #$5EFF     ; arm a real stack (hygiene, matches tests/halt.rs)
$8004  86 05         LDA  #$05       ; C1_IRQ_ENABLE (0x01) | DDR_ACCESS (0x04)
$8006  B7 FF 03      STA  $FF03      ; PIA0 CRB: enable the CB1 (field-sync) IRQ
$8009  1C EF         ANDCC #$EF      ; clear the I mask: unmask IRQ recognition
$800B  20 FE         BRA  *          ; the idle loop — this IS a BRA *, literally

$8100  B6 FF 02      LDA  $FF02      ; read PIA0 port B's DATA register: acks CB1
$8103  7C 04 00      INC  $0400      ; "I'm alive" — one tick per field-sync IRQ
$8106  3B            RTI
```

Vectors: `$FFFE`/`$FFFF` (RESET) point at `$8000`; `$FFF8`/`$FFF9` (IRQ)
point at `$8100`. As a 32K flat image with those bytes poked in (`ROM_SIZE
= 32 * 1024`, offsets relative to `$8000` exactly like `halt.rs::test_rom`),
`Machine::new(MachineConfig::default(), rom)` boots it on a CoCo 3 with no
ROM file and no license concerns anywhere near it.

Walk what each line does against code you've already read this chapter.
`LDA #$05; STA $FF03` writes PIA0's control register B, setting bit `0x01`
(`cr::C1_IRQ_ENABLE`) and bit `0x04` (`cr::DDR_ACCESS`) from the constants
table in §6.6; `write_control`'s masking of the two read-only flag bits
lets both of these land untouched. `ANDCC #$EF` clears the I bit in the
condition-code register, unmasking IRQ recognition — the same mask
`cpu.irq()` checks before actually vectoring anywhere.

`BRA *` is the idle loop, and it is worth appreciating how completely it
does nothing. Once the preamble has run, no instruction in this program
will ever execute again *except* in response to an interrupt. That is
precisely stock BASIC's own idle-loop design, per the war story, with a
counter standing in for "blink the cursor, scan the keyboard."

### Watch it fail, twice, before it works

Building this for real — not writing it and assuming it's right — turned
up two genuine bugs, both worth showing rather than silently editing away,
because both are realistic mistakes and both are direct, verified
demonstrations of facts this chapter and the wider course already claim.

**Attempt 1: forget the acknowledgment.** Drop the `LDA $FF02` line
entirely — CRB is armed, the idle loop spins, the handler just does
`INC $0400; RTI`. §6.6 already told you reading a PIA data register
clears its Cx1 flag, and that this is *why* `Bus::read` takes `&mut self`
(Chapter 1). Skip that read and the flag PIA0 latched at line 244 is never
cleared — so at the very next instruction boundary after `RTI` restores CC
(with I unmasked again), the CPU sees the same still-asserted IRQ line and
re-enters the handler immediately, forever, for the rest of the run.
Running three fields of this actually produces:

```
[no-ack] field 1: $0400 = 51
[no-ack] field 2: $0400 = 69
[no-ack] field 3: $0400 = 87
```

Not a frozen machine, which was §6.6's war story — this is the opposite
failure, an **interrupt storm**: 51 handler visits in field 1 alone against
the single visit a correct handler produces, and it never recovers, because
the flag, once missed, stays latched across field boundaries too. Every
field after the first racks up 786 visits — the counter only *appears* to
advance by 18 a field because `INC $0400` increments a single memory byte,
and 786 wraps to 18 mod 256. Two very different bugs — "no interrupt ever
fires" and "an interrupt fires continuously and never stops" — sit on
opposite sides of the exact same missing line of code, which is a good
reason to actually run a change like this rather than eyeball it.

**Attempt 2: acknowledge the wrong register.** Put `LDA $FF02` back, but
leave `CRB = $01` (just `C1_IRQ_ENABLE`, no `DDR_ACCESS`). This looks
fixed — there's an acknowledgment read right there — and it isn't:

```
[ddr-not-set] field 1: $0400 = 40
[ddr-not-set] field 2: $0400 = 139
[ddr-not-set] field 3: $0400 = 238
```

Not fixed at all. The reason is `MC6821::read_side`, and this is the point
to actually look at it, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs:113-132`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/pia.rs#L113-L132):

```rust
/// Read one of the 4 PIA registers (`addr & 0x03`). Reading a data register
/// clears that side's Cx1/Cx2 interrupt flags.
pub fn read(&mut self, reg: u8) -> u8 {
    match reg & 0x03 {
        0 => Self::read_side(&mut self.a),
        1 => self.a.control,
        2 => Self::read_side(&mut self.b),
        _ => self.b.control,
    }
}

fn read_side(port: &mut PIAPort) -> u8 {
    if port.control & cr::DDR_ACCESS != 0 {
        // Reading the peripheral data register clears the interrupt flags.
        port.control &= !(cr::C1_FLAG | cr::C2_FLAG);
        port.data()
    } else {
        port.ddr
    }
}
```

Register 2 is `$FF02`, port B's data address, and `read_side` is where the
acknowledgment is supposed to happen. But look at the guard: the flag
clearing only runs when the port's control register has `cr::DDR_ACCESS`
set. With that bit clear, the exact same address instead reads the *data
direction register* — a completely different piece of state that has
nothing whatsoever to do with interrupt flags — and returns without
touching `control`. The flag stays latched exactly as in attempt 1.

The storm is, if anything, slightly *slower* than attempt 1's, and the
arithmetic says why. The handler now carries an extra `LDA`, so each false
interrupt round-trip burns more cycles and fewer of them fit in a field: 40
visits in field 1 against attempt 1's 51, and 611 per field thereafter
against attempt 1's 786. The printed bytes invert that, for the same
mod-256 reason as attempt 1 — 611 wraps to 99 where 786 wraps to 18 — so
the counter that looks like it is climbing five times faster is in fact
being incremented less often.

This is precisely the situation Chapter 10's own third exercise asks you to
reason about in the abstract — "why does the DDR exist at all, and what
would break if PIA registers were read/write plain bytes?" — except that
here it isn't abstract at all. It is a live specimen, with real numbers, of
a "fixed" handler that silently does nothing.

**Attempt 3: `CRB = $05`.** `C1_IRQ_ENABLE | DDR_ACCESS`, both bits, so
`LDA $FF02` actually reaches the data register and clears the flag on the
way out of the handler:

```
[working] field 1: $0400 = 1
[working] field 2: $0400 = 2
[working] field 3: $0400 = 3
[working] field 4: $0400 = 4
[working] field 5: $0400 = 5
[working] field 6: $0400 = 6
```

Exactly one field-sync IRQ per field, every field, indefinitely — the
`$0400` counter after `run_field()` number *n* reads exactly *n*. This is
the version worth trusting, and it's the one behind §6.3's `run_until`
walkthrough and the histogram below.

Three programs, one control-register bit apart from each other, three
completely different behaviors. That is a fair sample of what debugging
peripheral code is like: the failures are not in the parts that look
complicated.

### Instructions per line, for real

§6.10's exercise 6.3 asks you to histogram instructions-per-line during a
boot; here's a worked answer using attempt 3's program, so you have a
known-good result to check your own instrumentation against before you
try it against real BASIC. Instrument `step_instruction` in a loop,
bucket `StepKind::Instruction` counts by `m.current_scanline()`, run one
field:

```
total retired instructions, field 1: 4971
  line   0: 18 instructions
  line   1: 19 instructions
  line 100: 19 instructions
  line 200: 19 instructions
  line 242: 19 instructions
  line 243: 19 instructions
  line 244: 19 instructions
  line 245: 13 instructions   <-- the IRQ detour lands here
  line 246: 19 instructions
  line 260: 19 instructions
  line 261: 19 instructions
distribution (instruction count -> how many lines had it): {13: 1, 18: 1, 19: 260}
```

Two numbers explain almost the whole histogram. `BRA` costs 3 cycles on the
6809, and NTSC's normal-speed line budget is 56 cycles from §6.2. Divide
and you get `56 / 3 ≈ 18.67`, which is exactly why 260 of the 262 lines
retire 19 `BRA` instructions apiece: eighteen full three-cycle spins plus a
partial one whose remaining cycles roll into the next line's budget. That
is §6.2's double-truncation arithmetic showing up as a histogram instead of
a formula, and it is a satisfying confirmation that the cycle budget is
being honored to the cycle rather than approximately.

Line 0 shows 18 instead of 19 because the field's first several
instructions are the one-time `LDS`/`LDA`/`STA`/`ANDCC` preamble rather
than `BRA`, and every one of those costs more than three cycles, leaving
less of line 0's budget for spinning.

And line 245 drops to 13. Two things are going on there, and both are
things this chapter has already explained. The dip lands one line *after*
the falling edge at 244 because the interrupt is recognized at the next
instruction boundary following the edge, never synchronously with it —
`end_of_line` raises the flag, and `step_cpu_unit` offers it to the CPU on
the following unit. And the magnitude of the dip is the cost of the detour:
the IRQ entry pushes all eight registers before the handler's first
instruction runs, and the three-instruction handler plus that stack frame
together cost far more than a `BRA`, so fewer total units fit inside that
line's fixed 56-cycle budget.

Nothing in that histogram is a bug. It is §6.2's arithmetic and §6.4's
ordering, rendered as a number you can count — which is a much better way
to believe a timing model than reading it.

## 6.8 The lab: booting real Color BASIC, headless, in a test

Everything so far has been either code or a synthetic program written to
exercise one mechanism. This section is the real thing.
[`crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs)
drives `run_field` from cold reset against a real 1980s ROM image, waits
for the sign-on banner, types `PRINT 2+2` one keystroke at a time, and
asserts on the answer — with no window, no GPU, and no human anywhere in
the loop. Walk it top to bottom, because every function it calls is one
you have now read.

**Loading the ROM.** `try_load` at
[`coco1_boot.rs:28-33`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs#L28-L33)
reads `roms/bas12.rom` relative to the workspace root. That is Color BASIC
1.2, the CoCo 1's ROM, *without* Extended Color BASIC — a deliberately
minimal target. `boot_machine` at
[`coco1_boot.rs:38-59`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs#L38-L59)
composes a 32K flat image out of it: `$FF` open-bus filler for the low 8K
where Extended BASIC would have lived, the real `bas12.rom` bytes at the
high 8K from `$A000` to `$BFFF`. Then it constructs a `Machine` configured
as a CoCo 1, NTSC, 32K of RAM, no monitor — a stock CoCo 1 has no RGB or
composite port, only the RF modulator into a television — and a plain
`VDGVariant::MC6847`.

**Driving time.** The test's core loop is almost embarrassingly plain, at
[`coco1_boot.rs:109-117`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs#L109-L117):

```rust
const MAX_FIELDS: usize = 1500;
let mut alive = false;
for _ in 0..MAX_FIELDS {
    m.run_field();
    if screen_contains(&mut m, "OK") && screen_contains(&mut m, "COLOR BASIC 1.2") {
        alive = true;
        break;
    }
}
```

That's it. `run_field()` is called up to 1500 times, which at 59.94 fields
per second is about twenty-five seconds of emulated wall-clock time and
generous headroom for a cold boot. After every field the test peeks at the
screen. There is no special-cased "wait for boot" hook, no
`run_until_prompt` helper, no ROM-specific knowledge in the loop at all.
`run_field` is the entire API surface a caller needs in order to make time
pass correctly, sync pulses and interrupts and all — which is the claim
this whole chapter has been building toward, stated as nine lines of
test code.

**Reading the screen without a framebuffer.** `screen_contains` at
[`coco1_boot.rs:61-63`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs#L61-L63)
calls `m.text_screen_lines()`, and that function does something worth
looking at, from
[`crates/coco-core/src/machine/video_mode.rs:92-112`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/src/machine/video_mode.rs#L92-L112):

```rust
pub fn text_screen_lines(&mut self) -> Vec<String> {
    match self.video_mode() {
        VideoMode::CocoText | VideoMode::CocoGraphics => {
            let base = self.legacy_display_base();
            (0..video::ROWS as u16)
                .map(|row| {
                    (0..video::COLS as u16)
                        .map(|col| {
                            let addr = base.wrapping_add(row * video::COLS as u16 + col);
                            video::decode_alpha_char(self.bus.read(addr))
                        })
                        .collect()
                })
                .collect()
        }
        // ...
    }
}
```

It does not read the RGBA framebuffer at all. It walks the legacy VDG text
screen directly out of RAM, through the bus — honoring the MMU, though a
CoCo 1 has none — and decodes each byte with `video::decode_alpha_char`,
the very same function the real renderer uses. That last detail is what
makes the test trustworthy: because the decoder is shared, the test can
never see something different from what a screen would actually show. A
private test-only decoder would be a second implementation of the same
table, and a second implementation is a second place to be wrong.

This is the headless-testability payoff `DESIGN.md` and Chapter 1 promised.
"The machine you can PEEK" is available to a `#[test]` function exactly as
it was available to `PEEK` statements typed at the BASIC prompt: 32 columns
by 16 rows of decoded ASCII, straight out of RAM, no pixels and no GPU
required.

**Typing.** Once the sign-on banner and `OK` prompt appear, the test types
`PRINT 2+2` by driving the actual keyboard matrix, Chapter 10's subject. It
holds each key down for three fields, then releases it for three more, at
[`coco1_boot.rs:69-90`](https://github.com/sperano/cocovm/blob/main/crates/coco-core/tests/coco1_boot.rs#L69-L90):

```rust
fn tap_char(m: &mut Machine, c: char) {
    let (pos, shift) =
        coco_core::keyboard::char_key(c).unwrap_or_else(|| panic!("no key for {c:?}"));
    if shift {
        m.bus.keyboard.set(coco_core::keyboard::SHIFT, true);
    }
    for _ in 0..3 {
        m.bus.keyboard.set(pos, true);
        m.run_field();
    }
    m.bus.keyboard.set(pos, false);
    m.bus.keyboard.set(coco_core::keyboard::SHIFT, false);
    for _ in 0..3 {
        m.run_field();
    }
}
```

Three fields is about fifty milliseconds of emulated time, which is roughly
how long a person's finger rests on a key — and, more to the point, long
enough for the ROM's own debounced scan loop to notice. That scan loop runs
off the very field-sync interrupt §6.6 spent the whole section explaining,
so this function only works because the interrupt chain works. Hold the key
for one field instead of three and the ROM will very likely miss it.

Then the test waits up to `ANSWER_FIELDS = 120` more fields for the string
`" 4"` to appear anywhere on screen, and asserts. The leading space is
Color BASIC's own formatting for a positive numeric result — the sign
column that a negative number would have used — and matching on it rather
than on a bare `"4"` avoids a false positive from the `2+2` still sitting
in the echoed input line.

> **Rust corner: `let ... else` for the early-out.** `boot_machine` opens
> with a `let`-`else` binding, which is the construct that makes the
> skip-if-absent pattern below read cleanly:
>
> ```rust
> let Some(bas) = try_load("bas12.rom") else {
>     eprintln!(
>         "skipping coco1_boot: bas12.rom not present in roms/ (see docs/coco12-plan.md \"ROM files\")"
>     );
>     return None;
> };
> ```
>
> A `let`-`else` binds the pattern's contents into the *enclosing* scope
> when the match succeeds — `bas` is a plain `Vec<u8>` for the rest of the
> function — and the `else` block must diverge, meaning it has to `return`,
> `break`, `continue`, or panic. The compiler enforces that; an `else`
> block that falls through is a compile error, because there would be no
> value to bind.
>
> The alternative, `if let Some(bas) = ... { /* forty lines */ } else {
> ... }`, pushes the entire happy path one level of indentation to the
> right and puts the interesting code inside a conditional. `let`-`else`
> inverts it: handle the absence, leave, and continue at the original
> indentation with the value in hand. When a function has several such
> preconditions the difference between the two styles is the difference
> between a flat function and a staircase.

### Run it yourself — and a note on what you'll actually see

```
cargo test -p coco-core --test coco1_boot
```

Be precise about what happens when the ROM is absent, because it's
an important lesson about test hygiene, not a problem with the test. Run
it, and you'll see:

```
test coco1_boots_color_basic_only_and_evaluates_print ... ok
```

That "ok" is **not** proof the boot succeeded. `bas12.rom` is not present
in `roms/` in a fresh checkout — `.gitignore` excludes `/roms/*` outright,
because real ROM dumps are copyrighted in a way this codebase's own source
is not. The course README's Appendix B spells out why the two get different
legal treatment. Even the "local resources present on this machine" section
of this course's `CLAUDE.md` lists only `coco3.rom` and `disk11.rom`, so
Color BASIC 1.2 specifically isn't guaranteed to be sitting around even on
an author's own machine.
`boot_machine` handles this by *not failing*: it prints a message, returns
`None`, and the test function returns early —

```rust
fn boot_machine() -> Option<(Machine, Vec<u8>)> {
    let Some(bas) = try_load("bas12.rom") else {
        eprintln!(
            "skipping coco1_boot: bas12.rom not present in roms/ (see docs/coco12-plan.md \"ROM files\")"
        );
        return None;
    };
    // ...
}

#[test]
fn coco1_boots_color_basic_only_and_evaluates_print() {
    let Some((mut m, _bas_rom)) = boot_machine() else {
        return;
    };
    // ... the entire test body above never runs
}
```

Cargo's test harness has no first-class "skipped" state for a plain `fn`
test. Getting one would require `#[ignore]`, which behaves differently in a
way that defeats the purpose: an ignored test doesn't run *at all* by
default, whether or not the ROM is present. So a test that early-returns
reports "ok" in exactly the same words as a test that ran to completion and
passed every assertion. Verify this for yourself, because it is the only
way to actually watch the skip happen:

```
cargo test -p coco-core --test coco1_boot -- --nocapture
```

```
skipping coco1_boot: bas12.rom not present in roms/ (see docs/coco12-plan.md "ROM files")
test coco1_boots_color_basic_only_and_evaluates_print ... ok
```

If you have a legally-obtained `bas12.rom` — Color BASIC 1.2, 8 KB — drop
it in `roms/` and re-run. Now `alive` and `answered` really are being
computed, and a full twenty-five-second-of-emulated-time cold boot,
keystroke injection, and screen assertion all happen inside a `cargo test`
invocation that takes a fraction of a second of *wall* time, because the
host CPU is thousands of times faster than 894,886 Hz.

Either way, you have now read, and can predict the exact behavior of,
every line standing between `Machine::new` and a working `PRINT 2+2` —
whether or not this particular checkout can prove it today.

## What's next

Every device this course has built so far — the CPU, the bus, the PIA's
Cx1 latch — has been necessary but, until this week, silent: correct
values with no clock driving them into view. `run_field` is what turns
"correct" into "alive," and every remaining chapter assumes it's running
underneath. From here on, "the machine" means something that ticks.

Chapter 7 starts Part III, video, and it starts by asking the question this
chapter deliberately left unopened: what does `render_scanline` — called
every single line, right after `hsync`, quoted but never explained in §6.5
— actually *do*? You already know precisely when it runs and how often,
which is more than half of what a video chapter usually has to establish
before it can begin.

The answer has a surprise in it. The CoCo 3 BASIC screen you are about to
reproduce is not drawn by the GIME's flagship native video hardware at all.
It is the old MC6847 VDG's text mode, faithfully imitated by a chip that
replaced it — one more example, after the SAM strobes of Chapter 5 and the
legacy PIA interrupt path of this week, of the machine's history outliving
its successor silicon.
